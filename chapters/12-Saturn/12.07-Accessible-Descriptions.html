Figure 12.1.1 Earth from Saturn

A natural color image from the Cassini spacecraft looking back toward the inner Solar System from behind Saturn.

Image Elements

  • The dark night side of Saturn fills the upper left of the image as a large black curve, outlined by a thin bright edge of sunlight.
  • Saturn's rings cross the upper right of the image in many thin bands of tan, brown, and gray.
  • A bright, thin white ring runs along the lower edge of the main rings, and a fainter ring curves across the dark blue space below it.
  • Near the bottom right, a white arrow points up to a tiny, pale blue dot. This dot is Earth, 1.4 billion kilometers away.

Takeaway

  • From the distance of Saturn, all of Earth appears as a single point of light, much smaller than Saturn's rings.

Figure 12.2.1 Saturn over Five Years

Five Hubble Space Telescope images of Saturn arranged in a diagonal line on a black background, showing how the tilt of the rings appears to change.

Image Elements

  • Each image shows the pale yellow planet with faint horizontal cloud bands, surrounded by its rings.
  • The images run from the lower left to the upper right in order of time, from 1996 to 2000.
  • In the lower left image, the rings are seen nearly edge-on, as a thin line across the planet's equator.
  • In each image moving up and to the right, the rings appear more open, showing more of their width.
  • In the upper right image, the rings are tilted at their widest angle, and the dark gap of the Cassini Division can be seen between the rings.

Takeaway

  • Saturn is tilted by 27°, so as it moves around its orbit, we see its rings from different angles.

Figure 12.2.2 Cloud Structure on Saturn

A close Cassini image of part of Saturn's northern hemisphere with the colors intensified to show the structure of its clouds.

Image Elements

  • The curved edge of the planet fills most of the image, with the north pole toward the upper right.
  • Wide cloud bands curve across the planet in alternating colors of pale green, blue, tan, and orange-brown.
  • Small spots and swirls of lighter color within the bands are storms.
  • The edges between the bands are smooth with a few ripples.
  • Near the bottom left, a dark band curves along the planet, below a bright, white curved region. The dark band is the shadow of the rings on the planet.

Takeaway

  • When the colors are enhanced, Saturn shows bands, zones, and storms similar to Jupiter, although with less contrast.

Figure 12.2.3 Saturn's Hexagon

A false color infrared image of Saturn's north pole at night, taken by the Cassini spacecraft.

Image Elements

  • Most of the image is red, showing infrared light from the clouds near the pole.
  • In the center, a band of clouds forms a six-sided shape, a hexagon, around the north pole.
  • Smaller round swirls and storms are inside and outside the hexagon.
  • A thin curve of blue at the top of the image is the edge of the planet against space.

Takeaway

  • Saturn's north pole is circled by a jet stream with a unique hexagonal shape.

Figure 12.3.1 Structure of Titan's Atmosphere

A chart showing how temperature and pressure change with height in the atmosphere of Saturn's moon Titan, with the layers of the atmosphere drawn in the background.

Chart Elements

  • The vertical axis on the left is Height above Titan's Surface (km), from 0 at the bottom to 600 at the top.
  • The vertical axis on the right is Pressure (bar), from 1.6 at the surface to 10-5 at about 400 km. Pressure decreases with height.
  • The horizontal axis is Temperature (K), from 80 on the left to 180 on the right.
  • The background is dark blue near the surface and becomes lighter blue with height. A rough, tan surface with mountains is at the bottom.

Temperature Line

  • A red line shows the temperature at each height.
  • At the surface, the temperature is about 94 K.
  • The temperature drops to a minimum of about 70 K at a height of about 50 km.
  • Above that, the temperature rises steeply to about 160 K at 300 km, and then stays near 165 K up to 600 km.

Atmospheric Layers

  • Clouds of methane or ethane near 80 km.
  • A region of possible particulate rain from about 220 to 300 km.
  • A thick photochemical haze near 300 km.
  • Nitrogen (N2) and methane (CH4) gas near 400 km.
  • A thin haze layer near 520 km.

Figure 12.3.2 Titan's Surface

Two parts showing Titan's surface: four panoramic views taken by the Huygens probe as it descended, and one view of the ground after landing.

Descent Views

  • On the left, four wide, orange-brown panels are stacked vertically, each flattened so that the full circle of the horizon is shown. Directions across the top are South, West, North, East, and South.
  • 150 km: A hazy orange view with a thin band of blue near the top. No surface details can be seen.
  • 15 km: Light and dark regions of the surface with rugged, hilly terrain can be seen.
  • 2 km: A bright, ridged highland with branching channels runs down toward smoother dark lowlands.
  • 0.4 km: A closer view of the same ridges and channels.

Surface View

  • On the right, a tall image of the flat ground after landing, lit by dim orange light.
  • Rounded, pale boulders cover the ground. The boulders are larger in the foreground and smaller toward the horizon.
  • The boulders are made of water ice.

Figure 12.3.3 Titan's Lakes

Two images of Titan's surface from the Cassini-Huygens mission, labeled (a) and (b).

Image (a)

  • On the left
  • A radar image with colors added.
  • Yellow-tan areas are rough, solid ground.
  • Dark blue and black patches of different sizes and irregular shapes are scattered across the image. These are smooth lakes of liquid methane and ethane.
  • A large dark lake fills the lower right corner.

Image (b)

  • On the right
  • A black and white mosaic of a bright, high region above a darker, smooth lowland.
  • Many narrow, dark, winding lines branch across the bright region and join together as they flow downhill, like a network of rivers.
  • The channels appear to be carved by flowing liquid hydrocarbons.

Figure 12.3.4 Enceladus

Two images of Saturn's moon Enceladus, labeled (a) and (b).

Image (a)

  • On the left
  • Enceladus is a bright white sphere against black space.
  • The upper part of the moon has many small, round craters.
  • Other regions are smooth and crossed by grooves and cracks.
  • Near the south pole at the bottom, several long, parallel blue-green lines are the tiger stripes. The color of the stripes is exaggerated. The stripes are warmer than their surroundings and are the source of geysers.

Image (b)

  • On the right
  • A satellite image of Great Britain, Ireland, and the coast of Western Europe, with green and brown land, blue water, and white clouds.
  • Enceladus is placed over the North Sea, to the right of Great Britain, at the same scale.
  • Enceladus is about as wide as Great Britain is from east to west at its widest, about 500 kilometers.

Figure 12.3.5 Geysers on Enceladus

A black and white Cassini image of the edge of Enceladus, lit from behind, with jets of material spraying into space.

Image Elements

  • The bright, rough, icy edge of the moon curves across the lower part of the image from the lower left to the right.
  • The rest of the moon below the edge is in dark shadow.
  • Above the edge, at least a dozen thin, bright jets rise into the black sky, fading as they get higher.
  • The jets begin at several points along the surface, lined up along the tiger stripes.

Takeaway

  • The geysers are salty water from below the surface escaping through cracks, which suggests that Enceladus has liquid water inside.

Figure 12.4.1 Four Ring Systems

A diagram comparing the ring systems and inner moons of the four giant planets, with each system drawn to a different scale.

Diagram Elements

  • Four rows, from top to bottom: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.
  • The left edge of the diagram represents the surface of each planet. Distance from the planet increases to the right.
  • A vertical dotted line on the right side marks the tidal stability limit. Inside this limit, to the left, the gravity of the planet can pull moons apart.
  • Rings are shown as shaded bars or thin vertical lines. Moons are shown as black dots, with larger dots for larger moons.

Ring Systems

  • Jupiter: one faint ring, shaded from white to red, ending with two small moons, inside the limit. One larger moon is just outside the limit.
  • Saturn
    • Wide rings labeled, from left to right, C (pink), B (tan), and A (red), followed by the narrow F ring.
    • Several small moons are within and just outside the rings, inside the limit.
    • Two moons are just outside the limit, and one large moon is farther out.
  • Uranus: nine narrow rings drawn as thin lines, with the wider epsilon ring (ε) as the outermost. Several small moons are among and beyond the rings, and two larger moons are outside the limit.
  • Neptune: a few narrow rings and a wide, faint green band that ends near the limit. Small moons are among the rings and one larger moon is outside the limit.

Takeaway

  • All four ring systems lie mostly inside the tidal stability limit, and only very small moons survive inside it.

Figure 12.4.2 Above and Below the Rings

Two black and white Cassini images of the same part of Saturn's rings, labeled (a) and (b).

Image (a)

  • On the left
  • The rings seen from above, the side lit by direct sunlight. A part of Saturn is in the lower right corner.
  • The rings curve from the upper left to the right.
  • The broad middle ring, the B Ring, is the brightest.
  • A dark gap, the Cassini Division, separates it from the gray outer A Ring.
  • The inner C Ring, closer to the planet, is dark and faint.

Image (b)

  • On the right
  • The rings seen from below, lit only by sunlight that passes through gaps between the ring particles. The dark shape of Saturn is at the top.
  • The pattern of brightness is reversed compared to Image (a).
  • The dense B Ring blocks most of the light and looks dark, while the Cassini Division and the thinner rings appear brighter.

Figure 12.4.3 Inside the Rings

An artist's illustration of what it might look like inside Saturn's rings.

Image Elements

  • Many pale blue and white chunks of ice float in space, from small grains to large boulders.
  • The chunks are gathered in loose clumps and long streams that stretch into the distance.
  • A large clump of icy boulders is in the front of the image, at the lower left.
  • The background is the golden-tan cloud bands of Saturn, with the curved edge of the planet at the left.

Takeaway

  • Saturn's rings are made mostly of pieces of water ice of many different sizes.

Figure 12.4.4 The F Ring and Shepherd Moons

Two black and white Cassini images of Saturn's narrow F Ring, labeled (a) and (b).

Image (a)

  • On the left
  • Two thin, bright lines run diagonally from the upper left to the lower right, coming together at the lower right. These are parts of the narrow F Ring.
  • Two small, bright, oval moons are near the ring, one near the center and one at the lower right. These are the shepherd moons Pandora, on the left, and Prometheus, on the right.

Image (b)

  • On the right
  • A closer view of the F Ring, curving across the left side of the image.
  • The ring has several separate strands, with one bright main strand surrounded by fainter, fuzzy strands.
  • A tiny moon, Pandora, 84 kilometers across, is to the right of the ring, near the bottom.

Takeaway

  • The gravity of the shepherd moons keeps the ring particles confined in a narrow ribbon.

Figure 12.5.1 Galileo's Sketches of the Moon

Five black ink drawings of the Moon in different phases, drawn by Galileo Galilei and published in 1610.

Image Elements

  • One drawing is at the top, with two rows of two drawings below it.
  • The dark part of the Moon in each drawing is filled with diagonal lines of shading.
  • Top: a thin crescent Moon, lit on the right side.
  • Middle row: the Moon about half lit. On the left, the right half is lit. On the right, the left half is lit.
  • Bottom row: two more views of the Moon about half lit, with the left half lit.
  • In each drawing, the boundary between the light and dark sides is jagged, with bright spots in the dark side near the boundary, showing mountains catching sunlight.
  • Several drawings show a large, round crater near the boundary, and gray patches on the lit side mark darker regions.

Takeaway

  • Galileo's drawings were the first to show that the Moon has mountains and craters, recording what he saw through his telescope before photography existed.

Figure 12.5.2 Mystic Mountain

A square oil painting by Dinah Jasensky of a nebula where stars are forming.

Image Elements

  • Swirling blue, teal, and purple gas fills the background, painted with loose, visible brushstrokes.
  • On the left, a large tower of glowing orange and dark red gas reaches up and to the right, with bright yellow-orange light on its upper edges.
  • On the right, two smaller clumps of dark red gas.
  • Many stars of different sizes are scattered across the painting, mostly white and blue, with a few pink and red. Some bright stars have four-pointed spikes of light.
  • A small cluster of blue-white stars is in the upper right.

Takeaway

  • The artist uses bold color and active brushstrokes to show the motion of gas in a star-forming region.

Figure 12.5.3 The Crucible

A tall oil painting by Dinah Jasensky showing a dying star and a stellar nursery in the same region of space.

Image Elements

  • The background is dark, with swirling clouds of gray, blue, and dusty pink gas running through it.
  • In the upper right, a bright pinkish-red star with long spikes of light is at the center of a spiral of pale gas that it has ejected.
  • Small orange-red stars are scattered through the middle of the painting.
  • In the lower left, a dense cluster of many bright white and pale yellow stars glows within blue-gray gas. This is a stellar nursery of hot young stars.

Takeaway

  • In astronomy, red stars are cooler and white stars are hotter, the opposite of how artists usually use red to suggest warmth and blue or white to suggest coolness.
