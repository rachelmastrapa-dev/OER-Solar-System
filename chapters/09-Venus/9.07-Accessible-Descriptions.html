Figure 9.1.1 Pioneer Venus

An ultraviolet image of Venus taken by the Pioneer Venus Orbiter, showing only the tops of the clouds that cover the entire planet.

Image Elements

  • Venus is a round globe centered on a black background.
  • The planet is tan and light brown, covered in streaks and swirls of cloud.
  • Darker brown bands curve across the upper middle of the planet, and lighter, feathery streaks sweep toward the bottom.
  • No part of the surface of Venus is visible through the clouds.

Summary

  • Ultraviolet light reveals patterns in the upper clouds of Venus that cannot be seen in visible light.
  • The clouds completely cover Venus, so the surface cannot be observed from above in ultraviolet or visible light.

Figure 9.1.2 Radar Map of Venus

A false-color radar map of one hemisphere of Venus made with data from the Magellan spacecraft, with colors showing elevation.

Color Key

  • Blue and purple are the lowest regions.
  • Green is middle elevation.
  • Brown, pink, and white are the highest regions.

Map Elements

  • Venus is shown as a globe on a black background.
  • Blue and purple lowlands are mostly near the top and bottom of the globe, at high latitudes.
  • Green middle elevations cover much of the planet.
  • A broad band of brown, pink, and white highlands stretches across the center of the globe, along the equator. This is the continent Aphrodite.
  • Thin, winding lines and ridges cross the highlands, showing a rough surface deformed by tectonic forces.

Summary

  • Radar can see through the clouds of Venus, so it can be used to map the surface.
  • On this hemisphere of Venus, the lowlands are at high latitudes and the highlands are near the equator.

Figure 9.1.3 Surface of Venus

Two wide-angle, visible light views of the surface of Venus taken by the Venera 13 lander, placed side by side.

Image Elements

  • The images are taken with a fisheye lens, so the horizon curves into the upper left and upper right corners.
  • The base of the lander, a curved metal ring with triangular teeth, runs along the bottom of both images.
  • A white, cup-shaped piece of the lander lies on the ground near the center of the left image.
  • A red and white striped bar, part of the lander, is on the lower right of the right image.
  • The ground is covered with flat, broken slabs of rock and dark, fine soil.
  • Everything in the images has an orange color.

Summary

  • The surface of Venus is made of flat rocks and dark soil.
  • The surface appears orange because the thick atmosphere of Venus absorbs the bluer colors of sunlight.

Figure 9.1.4 Ikaros Model

A model of the Japanese IKAROS solar sail spacecraft on display in a clear case.

Image Elements

  • The model is a thin, square sail standing on a black stand, tilted so that one corner points up.
  • The sail is shiny and gold-colored, with a small square hub at its center that holds a round, dark body.
  • Two dark lines run diagonally from corner to corner across the sail, crossing at the hub.
  • Thin purple and orange rectangles are arranged around the sail, forming a pattern of squares around the hub.
  • A small card with Japanese text and the word IKAROS sits on the lower right of the display case.

Summary

  • A solar sail is a large, thin, lightweight sheet that uses the pressure of sunlight to move a spacecraft through space.
  • IKAROS tested solar sail technology as it traveled past Venus.

Figure 9.2.1 Pancake Domes

A black and white radar image from the Magellan spacecraft showing several pancake-shaped volcanic domes on the surface of Venus.

Image Elements

  • The background is a gray, grainy plain crossed by thin, bright lines running diagonally from the lower left to the upper right.
  • Five round, flat-topped domes are spread across the middle of the image.
  • Three domes overlap in a cluster on the center left. The leftmost is faint and has small pits on it.
  • Two larger, bright domes overlap on the center right.
  • The domes have sharp, bright edges and a pattern of fine cracks on their flat tops.

Summary

  • Pancake domes form when thick lava erupts and piles up instead of flowing away.
  • This lava does not flow as easily as basalt, the type of lava common on Venus and Earth.

Figure 9.2.2 Fotla Corona

A black and white radar image from the Magellan spacecraft of the Fotla Corona, a large circular volcanic feature on Venus.

Image Elements

  • A large, round feature fills most of the left and center of the image.
  • The corona is outlined by many bright, curved lines in rings around its edge.
  • Three round pancake volcanoes lie inside the corona: one below the center, one above the center, and one to the left.
  • A fourth pancake volcano sits above and outside the corona, near the top of the image.
  • A short row of small bright spots runs across the lower part of the corona.
  • The plains around the corona are dark gray, crossed by a network of thin, bright cracks, especially on the right side of the image.

Summary

  • Coronae form when rising magma pushes up the crust from below, stretching and fracturing the surface.
  • Volcanoes often form in and around coronae.

Figure 9.2.3 Ridges and Cracks

A black and white radar image from the Magellan spacecraft of cracks on the Lakshmi Plains of Venus.

Image Elements

  • The background is a dark gray, grainy surface.
  • Two sets of cracks cross the whole image at right angles to each other.
  • First set:
    • Very faint, very straight lines that are closely spaced.
    • They run from the top left to the bottom right.
  • Second set:
    • Larger, brighter lines that are more widely and unevenly spaced.
    • They run from the top right to the bottom left.

Summary

  • The cracks show where the crust of Venus has been stretched.
  • Two sets of cracks at right angles to each other show that this area was stretched in different directions at different times.

Figure 9.3.1 Venus' Atmosphere

A diagram of the layers of the atmosphere of Venus, with a red curve showing how temperature changes with height.

Chart Elements

  • The vertical axis is labeled Height (km) and goes from 0 at the bottom to about 140 at the top, in increments of 10 km. The values 0, 50, and 100 are labeled.
  • The horizontal axis is labeled Temperature (K) and goes from 0 on the left to 700 on the right, in increments of 100 K.
  • A gray mountain labeled Volcano sits at the bottom of the chart, rising to a few kilometers in height.

Atmospheric Layers

  • From the bottom to the top:
  • Troposphere
    • From the ground to about 50 km.
    • The main gas is carbon dioxide, labeled CO2.
  • Clouds
    • A band of white clouds lies between about 35 and 55 km.
    • The clouds are labeled H2SO4 Clouds, which are made of sulfuric acid, and Sulfur? Clouds.
  • Stratosphere
    • From about 50 km to 80 km, shown in dark blue.
  • Mesosphere
    • From about 80 km to the top of the chart, shown in lighter blue.

Temperature Curve

  • The red curve begins at the ground at about 730 K.
  • The temperature drops steadily as height increases, reaching about 230 K at 50 km, near the top of the clouds.
  • Above the clouds, the temperature drops slowly to a minimum of about 150 K near 100 km.
  • The temperature then rises slightly to about 220 K at the top of the chart.

Summary

  • The temperature is much higher below the clouds, especially near the surface, because of the strong greenhouse effect on Venus.

Figure 9.4.1 Impact Craters on Venus

Two black and white radar images from the Magellan spacecraft showing impact craters on Venus.

Image (a)

  • On the left
  • Three large impact craters in the Lavinia region of Venus.
  • Each crater has a dark, round floor surrounded by a bright, rough ring of ejecta, material thrown out by the impact, with jagged, flower-like edges.
  • The largest crater is at the upper right, a second crater is at the upper left, and the smallest is at the lower left.
  • The surrounding plains are dark and smooth, with streaks of bright lines running through the center of the image.
  • A scale bar at the lower right reads 50 km, which is about the size of the largest crater.

Image (b)

  • On the right
  • The small, complex crater Stein in the center of the image.
  • The crater has three overlapping, dark craters surrounded by a bright, irregular field of ejecta.
  • The surrounding plains are dark gray and crossed by many thin, bright lines.
  • A scale bar at the lower right reads 10 km.

Summary

  • Rough surfaces, such as crater rims and ejecta, appear brighter in radar images than smooth lava plains.
  • The triple crater formed when an incoming asteroid broke apart while passing through the thick atmosphere of Venus.

Figure 9.5.1 The Pleiades and Venus

Five simulated images of the Pleiades and Venus from April 1 to April 5 in 2020.

  • In all images the Pleiades is a group of 6 stars against a solid black background. Venus is represented by a large white dot that is about half the size of the Pleiades. Throughout the images, Venus moves from below and to the right of the group to above and to the left.
    • April 1: Venus is a short distance below and to the right of the Pleiades.
    • April 2: Venus has moved closer to and is directly below the Pleiades.
    • April 3: Venus is now inside the Pleiades, covering two stars on the left side.
    • April 4: Venus is a little above and to the left of the Pleiades.
    • April 5: Venus has increased its distance upwards and to the left from the Pleiades.
