Figure 15.1.1 Doppler Method

A diagram showing how the wobble of a star caused by an orbiting planet can be detected with a telescope.

Diagram Elements

  • A telescope on a tripod is at the lower left, on a dark blue background, pointing toward the upper right.
  • At the upper right, a yellow star is shown at two positions on a small orange oval, on opposite sides of an orange x that marks the center of mass. White arrows on the oval show the star moving around the x.
  • A wavy blue line connects the telescope to the star on the left side of the oval, where the star is moving toward the telescope.
  • A wavy red line, with longer waves, connects the telescope to the star on the right side of the oval, where the star is moving away from the telescope.
  • The text between the lines reads Doppler shift due to stellar wobble.
  • Below the star, a small blue dot labeled Unseen planet moves to the right along its path, shown by an arrow.

Takeaway

  • A planet's gravity makes its star move in a small orbit, so the star's light shifts toward blue and then red. Measuring this shift reveals the planet, even when the planet cannot be seen.

Figure 15.1.2 Planet Transits

A diagram showing how a planet passing in front of its star changes the star's brightness.

Diagram Elements

  • At the top, a yellow star is surrounded by a blue oval showing the orbit of a planet. An arrow shows the planet moving left to right across the front of the star.
  • The planet is shown as a dark blue dot at three positions, labeled 1, 2, and 3.
  • Position 1: the planet is just to the left of the star.
  • Position 2: the planet is just starting to cross the edge of the star.
  • Position 3: the planet is fully in front of the star.

Light Curve

  • At the bottom, a chart with a vertical axis labeled Brightness and a horizontal axis labeled Time, with no numbers.
  • An orange line labeled Light curve shows the brightness of the star over time.
  • Dashed lines connect each position of the planet to the light curve.
  • At position 1, the brightness is constant. At position 2, the brightness begins to drop. At position 3, the brightness is at its lowest.
  • After the planet passes the star, the brightness rises back to its original level.

Takeaway

  • When a planet crosses in front of its star, it blocks a small amount of the star's light, causing a dip in the light curve.

Figure 15.2.4 Sizes and Masses of Exoplanets

A chart comparing the radii and masses of exoplanets to models of planets made of different materials.

Chart Elements

  • The vertical axis is labeled Planet Radius (Earth Radii) and goes from 0 to 20 in increments of 5.
  • The horizontal axis is labeled Planet Mass (Earth Masses) and goes from 0 to more than 1000. The scale is logarithmic.
  • The legend shows teal triangles for Planets in our solar system and red dots for Exoplanets with known masses and radii.

Model Curves

  • Four blue curves show the expected size of a planet of each mass for different materials. From bottom to top they are labeled Iron, Rock, Water, and Hydrogen.
  • Iron: rises from below 1 Earth radius to about 2.7 Earth radii at the highest masses.
  • Rock: rises from about 1 Earth radius to about 4 Earth radii near 1000 Earth masses, then levels off.
  • Water: rises from just above 1 Earth radius to about 6 Earth radii near 1000 Earth masses, then curves down slightly.
  • Hydrogen: rises from about 2.5 Earth radii to a peak of about 13 Earth radii near 1000 Earth masses, then curves down.

Data

  • Two teal triangles near 1 Earth radius and 1 Earth mass are Earth and Venus. Two near 4 Earth radii and 15 Earth masses are Uranus and Neptune. One near 9 Earth radii and 100 Earth masses is Saturn, and one near 11 Earth radii and 300 Earth masses is Jupiter.

  • A large cluster of red dots lies between 9 and 20 Earth radii and between 50 and 3000 Earth masses. Many of these dots are above the Hydrogen curve.

Takeaway

  • Many exoplanets have masses and sizes like the outer planets, but some are much larger than even a pure hydrogen planet of the same mass would be.

Figure 15.3.1 The Habitable Zone

An illustration comparing the habitable zones around three types of stars, on a starry black background.

Image Elements

  • Three stars are stacked from top to bottom, each at the center of a set of flat, oval orbits seen at an angle.
  • Around each star, the colors change from red near the star, to green, to blue farther out. The green band is the habitable zone, where liquid water could exist on a planet's surface. Closer in, in the red region, it is too hot, and farther out, in the blue region, it is too cold.
  • Top, labeled Hotter Stars: a blue-white star with a large red region, and a wide green band far from the star.
  • Middle, labeled Sunlike Stars: a yellow star with a smaller red region, and a green band at a medium distance. A few small planets are on the orbits.
  • Bottom, labeled Cooler Stars: a red star with a small red region, and a narrow green band close to the star.

Takeaway

  • The hotter the star, the larger and farther away its habitable zone. Cooler stars have small habitable zones close to the star.

Figure 15.4.1 Planetary Formation and Cooling

A diagram showing the stages of geological history of the terrestrial planets, with each world placed at its present stage.

Stages

  • A list of stages on the left, with time increasing from top to bottom:
    • Accretion, heating, differentiation
    • Formation of solid crust, heavy cratering
    • Widespread mare-like volcanism
    • Reduced volcanism, possible plate tectonics
    • Mantle solidification, end of tectonic activity
    • Cool interior, no activity

Worlds

  • On the right, on a black background titled Geological history of the terrestrial planets.
  • Five worlds hang from vertical white bars of different lengths, arranged from left to right by size. Each bar ends at the stage the world has reached.
  • The Moon, the smallest, has the longest bar, ending at Cool interior, no activity.
  • Mercury's bar ends at Mantle solidification, end of tectonic activity.
  • Mars's bar ends between Reduced volcanism and Mantle solidification.
  • Venus and Earth, the largest, have the shortest bars, ending at Reduced volcanism, possible plate tectonics.

Takeaway

  • Smaller worlds cool faster, so they have passed through more of the geological stages than larger worlds like Earth and Venus.
