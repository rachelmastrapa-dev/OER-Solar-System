Figure 14.1.1 Asteroids in the Solar System

A computer-generated diagram of the inner Solar System, seen from above, showing the positions of all asteroids known in 2006.

Diagram Elements

  • The Sun is a yellow-white circle at the center, on a black background.
  • The orbits of Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are blue circles close to the Sun, with each planet shown as a small colored dot.
  • A large blue circle near the edge of the diagram is the orbit of Jupiter, with Jupiter shown as an orange-brown dot at the lower left.

Asteroids

  • Thousands of white dots form a wide, thick ring between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter. This is the asteroid belt.
  • A few colored dots are scattered inside the orbit of Mars, among the inner planets.
  • Green dots form a cluster on Jupiter's orbit at the far left, labeled Trojans.
  • Green dots form a second cluster on Jupiter's orbit at the lower right, labeled Greeks.
  • Orange dots, labeled Hildas, are scattered just inside Jupiter's orbit, outside the main belt.

Takeaway

  • Most asteroids orbit the Sun between Mars and Jupiter, and Jupiter's gravity controls groups of asteroids along its orbit. The dots are much larger than the real asteroids, so the belt is mostly empty space.

Figure 14.1.2 Asteroids Types and Locations

A chart of how common three types of asteroids are at different distances from the Sun.

Chart Elements

  • The vertical axis is labeled Percent of Total and goes from 0 at the bottom to 100 at the top.
  • The horizontal axis is labeled Distance from Sun (AU) and goes from 1 to 5 in increments of 1.
  • The legend at the top shows a dashed black line for Differentiated and a solid red line for Primitive.

Curves

  • S-type (stony or silicaceous), solid red: begins at about 20% at 1.3 AU, peaks at about 65% at 2.2 AU, and falls to about 5% at 3.7 AU.
  • C-type (carbonaceous), solid red: begins at about 18% at 1.6 AU, peaks at about 90% at 3.2 AU, and falls to about 17% at 5 AU.
  • M-type (metallic), dashed black: begins near 7% at 1.9 AU, peaks at about 15% at 2.5 AU, and falls to near 0% at 3.4 AU.

Takeaway

  • Stony S-type asteroids are most common in the inner asteroid belt, carbon-rich C-type asteroids are most common in the outer belt, and metallic M-type asteroids are rare everywhere.

Figure 14.1.12 Discovery of Near-Earth Asteroids

A chart titled Near-Earth Asteroids Discovered, showing the total number of near-Earth asteroids found over time.

Chart Elements

  • The vertical axis is labeled Cumulative Number Discovered and goes from 0 to 15,000 in increments of 5000.
  • The horizontal axis is labeled Discovery Date and goes from 1980 to about 2015 in increments of 10 years.
  • Three shaded areas are stacked on top of each other. The legend shows dark blue for All sizes, yellow for 140 m+, and red for 1 km+.

Trends

  • All three areas stay close to zero until the late 1990s.
  • Red, 1 km+: rises slowly after the late 1990s and levels off near 900 by about 2015.
  • Yellow, 140 m+: rises steadily after the late 1990s to about 6,400 by about 2015.
  • Dark blue, All sizes: rises faster and faster after the late 1990s, reaching about 12,000 by about 2015.

Takeaway

  • The number of known near-Earth asteroids has grown rapidly since the late 1990s, while most of the largest ones, over 1 kilometer, have probably already been found.

Figure 14.4.2 Parts of a Comet

A diagram of the main parts of a comet, not to scale, on a black background.

Diagram Elements

  • The Nucleus (1-10 km) is a small black dot near the bottom center of the comet.
  • The Coma (105 km) is a small, bright white cloud around the nucleus.
  • The Ion tail is a thin, straight, blue-white line that goes straight up from the coma.
  • The Dust tail is a wide, speckled band that curves away from the coma toward the right.
  • A large, pale blue, U-shaped glow surrounds the coma and both tails. This is the hydrogen envelope.
  • A red arrow labeled To Sun points straight down from the nucleus.
  • A red arrow labeled Comet's motion points down and to the left, opposite to the curve of the dust tail.

Takeaway

  • The tiny nucleus produces a much larger coma and tails. The ion tail points away from the Sun, while the dust tail curves behind the comet's motion.

Figure 14.4.6 Comet Orbit and Tail

A diagram showing the direction of a comet's tail at different points in its orbit.

Diagram Elements

  • The Sun is a yellow circle near the left end of a long, blue oval labeled Comet's orbit, on a black background.
  • An arrow on the orbit shows that the comet travels from the upper right, around the left end near the Sun, and back toward the right along the bottom.
  • The comet is shown as a white dot with a short white tail at six positions on the orbit.

Comet Tails

  • At every position, the tail points directly away from the Sun.
  • Far from the Sun, at the right side of the orbit, the tails are very short.
  • The tail is longest at the left end of the orbit, where the comet is closest to the Sun.
  • On the way toward the Sun, along the top, the tail trails behind the comet. On the way out, along the bottom, the tail goes ahead of the comet.

Takeaway

  • A comet's tail always points away from the Sun, not behind the comet, and it is longest when the comet is closest to the Sun.

  • Pluto's surface includes several different geological regions next to each other.
