Closest to the Sun

Since Mercury's orbit is so small, it only appears very close to the Sun when viewed from Earth. This means it is only visible right before sunrise or right after sunset when it is far from the Sun in our sky. The last section in this chapter will review the history of humans observing Mercury.

Observations from Earth

The planet Mercury is similar to the Moon in many ways. Like the Moon, it has no atmosphere, and its surface is heavily cratered. Mercury is the nearest planet to the Sun and has the shortest orbital period, 88 Earth days. It also has the highest average orbital speed, 48 km/s.

The semi-major axis, or average distance from the Sun, of Mercury's orbit, is 58 million kilometers, or 0.39 AU. However, because its orbit has the high eccentricity of 0.206, Mercury's actual distance from the Sun varies from 46 million kilometers at perihelion, when it is closest to the Sun, to 70 million kilometers at aphelion, the farthest Mercury can be from the Sun.

Mercury's Strange Rotation

Studies of Mercury's indistinct surface markings were once thought to indicate that the planet kept one face to the Sun, as the Moon does to Earth. For many years, it was widely believed that Mercury's rotation period was equal to its orbital period of 88 days, making one side perpetually hot while the other was always cold.

Radar observations of Mercury in the mid-1960s, however, showed conclusively that Mercury does not keep one side fixed toward the Sun. If a planet is turning, one side seems to be approaching Earth while the other is moving away from it. If a radar signal is sent to Mercury, such as in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\), it reflects off of its surface and returns to Earth. The signal reflecting back from the center of Mercury will have the same frequency as the original. The signal from the side of Mercury that is moving away from Earth will be shifted to lower freqency and the side moving toward the Earth will be shifted to a higher freqency. This shift in frequency caused by movement is called the Doppler Effect. The shifting from the sides results in a broader range of frequencies in the returned signal compared to the original transmitted signal. The resulting Doppler shift spreads or broadens the precise transmitted radar-wave frequency into a range of frequencies in the reflected signal. The amount of broadening provides an exact measurement of the rotation rate of the planet.

Measuring Mercury's rate of rotation relative to distant stars, astronomers calculate that Mercury's rotation period compared to distant stars is is 59 days. This means that Mercury's rotation rate is just two-thirds of the planet's orbital period. Anyother way to describe it is that Mercury rotates three times for each two orbits around the Sun. Since this discover, astronomers found that a situation where the rotation period and orbital period are in a 2:3 ratio is stable.

Mercury, being close to the Sun, is very hot on its daylight side; but because it has no appreciable atmosphere, it gets very cold during the long nights. The temperature on the surface climbs to 700 K (430 °C) at noontime. After sunset, however, the temperature drops, reaching 100 K (-170 °C) just before dawn. It is even colder in craters near the poles that receive no sunlight at all. The range in temperature on Mercury is thus 600 K (or 600 °C), a greater difference than on any other planet.

A Day on Mercury

Here on Earth, we take for granted that days are much shorter than years. There is only a four minute difference between the length of Earth's sideral day, the time it takes to rotate relative to the stars, and Earth's solar day, the time it takes to rotate relative to the Sun. This is not the case on Mercury. While Mercury rotatation period is 59 Earth days, the time for the Sun to return to the same place in Mercury's sky is 176 Earth days. The high eccenticity of Mercury's orbit means that its distance from the Sun varies significantly during each Mercury year. By Kepler's law, the planet moves fastest in its orbit when closest to the Sun.

Let's examine how this affects the way we would see the Sun in the sky during one 176-Earth-day cycle. We'll look at the situation as if we were standing on the surface of Mercury in the center of Caloris Basin. Mercury is most distant from the Sun at sunrise. This means the rising Sun looks smaller in the sky, although still more than twice the size it appears from Earth. As the Sun rises higher and higher, it looks bigger and bigger as Mercury is getting closer to the Sun in its eccentric orbit. At the same time, the apparent motion of the Sun slows down as Mercury's faster motion in orbit begins to catch up with its rotation.

At noon, the Sun is now three times larger than it looks from Earth and hangs almost motionless in the sky. As the afternoon wears on, the Sun appears smaller and smaller, and moves faster and faster in the sky. Sunset is 88 Earth days after sunrise, or one Mercury year. The Sun is back to its smallest apparent size as it dips out of sight. Then it takes another Mercury year before the Sun rises again.

Astronomers call locations like the Caloris Basin the hot longitudes on Mercury because the Sun is closest to the planet at noon, just when it is lingering overhead for many Earth days. This makes these areas the hottest places on Mercury.

Spacecraft

The first close-up look at Mercury came in 1974, when the US spacecraft Mariner 10 passed 9500 kilometers from the surface of the planet and transmitted more than 2000 photographs to Earth, revealing details with a resolution down to 150 meters. Since Mariner 10 was a flyby mission, the resulting map of Mercury's surface was incomplete. Sadly the map stayed that way for 30 years, until NASA launched the MErcury Surface, Space ENvironment, GEochemistry, and Ranging, or MESSENGER mision in 2004. After enting orbit in 2011 MESSENGER was able to make a complete visible, topographic, and spectroscopic maps of Mercury's surface. It ended its life in 2015, when it was commanded to crash into the surface of the planet.

MESSENGER instruments measured the surface composition and mapped past volcanic activity. One of its most important discoveries was the verification of water ice, previously detected by Earth-based radar, in craters near the poles, similar to the situation on the Moon. It also found organic compounds mixed with the water ice.
