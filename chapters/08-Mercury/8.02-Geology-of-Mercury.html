Another Moon

At first glance, Mercury could be easily mistaken for the Moon. After careful examination, some differences, such as the lack of maria on Mercury, become apparent. To review, we have copied in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) which compares the properties of Mercury and the Moon. Mercury's mass is one-eighteenth that of Earth, but it is almost four and a half times more massive than the Moon. Mercury is the smallest planet, having a diameter of 4878 kilometers, less than half that of Earth. Mercury is roughly one and half times the size of the Moon. How can Mercury be so much more massive than the Moon what it is only a bit larger? Mercury's density is 5.4 g/cm3, much greater than the density of the Moon, indicating that the composition of those two objects differs substantially.

Mercury's composition is one of the most interesting things about it and makes it unique among the planets. Mercury's high density tells us that it must be composed largely of heavier materials such as metals. The most likely models for Mercury's interior suggest a metallic iron-nickel core amounting to 60% of the total mass, with the rest of the planet made up primarily of silicates. The core has a diameter of 3500 kilometers and extends out to within 700 kilometers of the surface. We could think of Mercury as a metal ball the size of the Moon surrounded by a rocky crust 700 kilometers thick. Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) is a diagram of Mercury's interior indicating that the core (yellow) is the largest layer in the planet, with a layer of mantle (red) above the core. Mercury's crust (pink) is very thin compared to the other layers.

Unlike the Moon, Mercury does have a weak magnetic field. The existence of this field is consistent with the presence of a large metal core, and it suggests that at least part of the core must be liquid in order to generate the observed magnetic field. To review, magnetism is an effect of moving electric charges. In atoms of metals, the outer electrons are easier to dislodge and they can form a current when the metal is in liquid form and can flow.

The Surface of Mercury

Mercury's surface strongly resembles the Moon in appearance. Most of the mercurian features have been named in honor of artists, writers, composers, and other contributors to the arts and humanities. Among the named craters are Bach, Shakespeare, Tolstoy, and Van Gogh, and Scott Joplin. It is covered with thousands of craters and larger basins up to 1300 kilometers in diameter. Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\) is an image of Caloris Basin. Larger than the state of Texas, Caloris has a diameter of 1,525 km and is surrounded by mountains that are over 1.5 km high. The smooth plains caused by post-impact lava flows in the interior of the basin have an area of almost two million square kilometers. The impact that formed it occurred roughly 3.8 billion years old. Signs of its extreme age include the lack of a sharply defined rim and the numerous crater scattered across it.

The MESSENGER spacecraft measured the topography of Mercury using a laser altimeter. A simple and inexpensive instrument, the laser altimeter sends light to the surface and measures the time the late takes to return. With enough orbits around a planet, astronomers can compile the altimeter data into the topographic maps in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\). This map is overlaid on a visible image of Mercury so that astronomers can correlate the altimeters data to geological structures. The lowest regions are purple and blue, the middle region is green and the highest regions are yellow and red. The difference in elevation between the lowest and highest regions shown here is roughly 10 kilometers.

The center of the map is Mercury's North Pole. The area near the pole is surrounded by low, smooth plains that have fewer craters than the higher topography regions surrounding them. These plains were created by lava flows that covered ancient craters. There are multiple smaller craters near the pole that are permanently shadowed and contain water ice. Around the perimeter of the map, near Mercury's equator, there are more than 20 medium size, deep craters. They are mostly in the medium level, green, regions, but some of them appear in the highest, yellow and red, regions. Hundreds of tiny craters are scattered over the surface.

There is no evidence of plate tectonics on Mercury. However, the planet's distinctive long scarps, like the one in Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\), sometimes cut across craters. This means the scarps must have formed later than the craters. These long, curved cliffs appear to have their origin in the slight compression of Mercury's crust. Apparently, at some point in its history, the planet shrank, wrinkling the crust, and it must have done so after most of the craters on its surface had already formed. This shrinkage must have taken place after the Solar System's early period of heavy bombardment.

Example: Densities of Worlds

The average density of a body equals its mass divided by its volume. For a sphere, density is:

\[\text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3} \nonumber\]

Astronomers can measure both mass and radius accurately when a spacecraft flies by a body.

Using the information in this chapter, we can calculate the approximate average density of the Moon.

Answer

For a sphere,

\[\text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3} = \frac{7.35 \times 10^{22} \text{ kg}}{4.2 \times 5.2 \times 10^{18} \text{ m}^3} = 3.4 \times 10^{3} \text{ kg/m}^3 \nonumber\]

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) gives a value of 3.3 g/cm3, which is 3.3 x 103 kg/m3.
