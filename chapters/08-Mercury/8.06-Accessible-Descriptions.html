Figure 8.1.1 Radar Measures Rotation

A diagram showing how a radar signal sent from Earth and reflected off a rotating planet can be used to measure the planet's rotation rate.

Diagram Elements

  • A portion of Earth is at the lower left, with a large radar dish on its surface pointing up and to the right.
  • A light blue beam labeled Transmitted signal travels from the dish to a gray sphere at the upper center labeled Target planet.
  • Five black arrows point from the planet back down toward Earth, spreading out slightly. These are labeled Reflected signal.

Signal Graphs

  • Four graphs are stacked vertically on the right. Each graph shows the strength of a signal as a peak. The horizontal axis at the bottom is labeled Frequency, with an arrow showing that frequency increases to the right.
  • Top graph:
    • Labeled Transmitted signal.
    • A tall, very narrow blue peak, showing the signal covers a very small range of frequencies.
  • Second graph:
    • Labeled Return with no rotation.
    • A tan peak that is tall and narrow, almost identical in shape to the transmitted signal.
  • Third graph:
    • Labeled Return with slow rotation.
    • A tan peak that is shorter and wider at the base than the previous peaks.
  • Bottom graph:
    • Labeled Return with faster rotation.
    • A tan peak that is the shortest and widest of the four, spreading over a broad range of frequencies.

Summary

  • A rotating planet has one side moving toward Earth and one side moving away, so the reflected signal is Doppler shifted to both higher and lower frequencies.
  • The faster the planet rotates, the wider the range of frequencies in the returned signal, so the width of the returned peak measures the rotation rate.

Figure 8.2.1 Mercury's Internal Structure

A cutaway diagram of Mercury showing its large metallic core and thin rocky outer layers.

Diagram Elements

  • Mercury is shown as a sphere. The left side shows the gray, cratered surface of the planet.
  • A wedge has been cut out of the right side to show the interior.
  • A thin outer layer of rock, the mantle and crust, is shown in red.
  • Inside the red layer is a large yellow sphere representing the metallic core. The core takes up most of the planet's interior, extending roughly three-quarters of the way from the center to the surface.

Summary

  • Compared to its size, Mercury has a very large metal core, with only a thin rocky layer above it.

Figure 8.2.2 Caloris Basin

A black and white image of Caloris Basin, a large impact basin on Mercury, taken in visible light by the MESSENGER spacecraft.

Image Elements

  • A large, roughly circular basin fills most of the image.
  • The floor of the basin is smooth and slightly lighter gray than its surroundings. It was flooded by lava after the impact.
  • Dozens of small craters, and one medium-sized crater near the center, are scattered across the smooth floor of the basin.
  • The edge of the basin is not a sharp rim. It is a ring of rough, hilly terrain.
  • Outside the basin, the surface is rough and heavily cratered, with craters of many sizes.

Summary

  • Caloris Basin is very old. Its rim has been worn down, and many younger craters formed on its floor after the lava flows.

Figure 8.2.3 Mercury's Topography

A false color topographic map of Mercury's northern hemisphere, made from laser altimeter data collected by the MESSENGER spacecraft. The map is a circle on a black background.

Color Scale

  • Colors show the height of the surface.
  • Purple and blue are the lowest regions.
  • Green is middle height.
  • Yellow, orange, and red are the highest regions.
  • The difference in height between the lowest and highest regions is roughly 10 kilometers.

Map Features

  • The center of the map is Mercury's North Pole.
  • A large blue and purple region of low, smooth plains stretches across the center of the map. These plains have fewer craters than the surrounding regions.
  • The low plains are surrounded by higher green, yellow, and red regions that are heavily cratered.
  • Near the edges of the map, closer to Mercury's equator, more than 20 medium-sized, deep craters appear as round, purple spots, many of them inside green regions.
  • Hundreds of tiny craters are scattered across the whole surface.

Summary

  • Lava flows created the low, smooth plains near the North Pole, covering older craters, while the higher regions remain heavily cratered.

Figure 8.2.4 Scarp on Mercury

A black and white image of a long cliff, called a scarp, on the surface of Mercury.

Image Elements

  • The surface is gray and covered with craters of many sizes.
  • The scarp is a long, winding cliff that runs from the top center of the image down to the bottom.
  • Near the top of the image, the scarp passes through a large oval crater, cutting across the crater's floor and rim.
  • The scarp is nearly 1 kilometer high and more than 100 kilometers long.

Summary

  • Because the scarp cuts across craters, it must have formed after those craters. Scarps like this formed when Mercury shrank and its crust was compressed.

Figure 8.4.1 Syzygy

A photograph of three planets lined up close together in the sky after sunset above the telescopes of La Silla Observatory in Chile.

Sky

  • The sky near the horizon glows bright orange from the sunset and fades to dark blue toward the top of the image.
  • Three points of light appear close together in the upper center of the image.
  • Jupiter is at the top.
  • Venus, the brightest of the three, is at the lower left.
  • Mercury, the faintest of the three, is at the lower right.

Ground

  • Dark mountain ridges stretch along the horizon.
  • In the foreground, several white and silver round telescope domes sit on a dark hilltop, connected by roads outlined with small green lights.

Summary

  • An alignment of three planets like this, called a syzygy, happens only once every several years and can be seen with the naked eye.
