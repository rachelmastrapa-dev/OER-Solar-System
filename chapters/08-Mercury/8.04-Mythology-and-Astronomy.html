Science and Society - Mythology and Astronomy

Naked-eye planet observations have long been a source of interest and wonder, particularly due to their bright appearance and curious motion across the sky. In the case of conjunctions, or alignments, naked-eye planets can offer striking views, such as the syzygy in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). Before the invention of electricity (and subsequent effects of light pollution) and telescopes, humans all around the world were able to see these objects wander across the night sky with just their eyes.

As mentioned in the chapter on Night Sky Navigation, numerous cultures have formed their own unique set of constellations and mythologies to interpret the heavenly bodies. The five naked-eye planets of Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn were named as "wanderers" in western mythology, due to their constant motion against the static background stars. The Sun and Moon are also considered "planets" as they wander through the zodiac constellations each month. Uranus and Neptune are figures that appear in Greek and Roman mythology, but the planets themselves were not discovered until the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Mercury is an inferior planet, meaning it orbits closer to the Sun than Earth, and can only be seen near sunrise or sunset. Its close proximity to the Sun makes it very difficult to spot, even in a cloudless sky. In terms of its motion, Mercury alternates between prograde and retrograde much more often than any other object in the sky, as its entire orbit around the Sun is only 88 days. Mercury spends roughly three months in prograde, then about three weeks in retrograde before switching back to prograde once more. For comparison, Saturn's apparent motion across the sky only puts it in retrograde about once per year. Interestingly, these physical characteristics appear in multiple interpretations of mythology about Mercury.

In Greek and Roman mythology, Mercury is associated with Hermes, who is a messenger of the Gods. Mercury's winged sandals (talaria) and winged hat (petasus) show his speed as messenger. The rooster, which announces the passage from night to day, was sacred to him. The ram stood for wealth and herds, and the tortoise was linked to him because of the lyre he made from its shell. While not all of these symbols have a clear physical connection, the frequent appearance of wings could be interpreted as a connection to Mercury's speed around the Sun, and the rooster could hold some association with a dawn and dusk appearance.

Another example comes from Mayan astronomy. Researchers and translators of the Popul Vuh (Quiché Mayan book of creation), suggest a connection between owls and the planet Mercury, as messengers from the underworld. As described in the book, the behavior of owls matches the movement of planet Mercury as a morning and evening star that has a waxing and waning appearance. It is thought that the appearance of the planet only in the early morning and evenings corresponds to a similar behavior of owls. Further research of the Dresden Codex (Mayan calendar) confirms that the Maya had a strong understanding of Mercury's movements, despite limited technology and the difficult task of tracking a planet so close to the Sun.

If you take time to study these ancient stories, you'll find many connections of the physical world with the characters and other aspects of their mythologies. The planets were not seen in great detail as we are able to see them today, but rather were associated by their movement or general appearance in comparison to the rest of the night sky. Even today we can look up at the night sky and see largely the same patterns and behavior of the planets as ancient civilizations were seeing. As humans have created endless stories and myths of the celestial bodies, we all share the same physical reality and view from our home planet Earth.
