An Inventory

The Solar System consists of the Sun, the planets, and their moons and rings, and smaller objects such as dwarf planets, asteroids, and comets. Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) lists the objects in the Solar System in order of percentage of the total mass of the Solar System. While it is not possible to place the entire Solar System on scale to measure its mass, scientists can calculate the mass of objects using physics and math. We will discuss how this is done when we discuss the orbits of the planets. Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) includes the many different types of objects in the Solar System.

In Table \(\PageIndex{1}\), the Sun takes up 99.8% of the total matter in our planetary system and is by far the most massive object in the Solar System. Jupiter contains only 0.1% of the total mass of the Solar System, while all of the other planets and dwarf planets add up to 0.04%. The remaining 0.06% includes all of the remaining objects in the Solar System: moons, asteroids, comets, and dust. Astronomers can only estimate the total mass of these objects since there are probably many small objects that have not been discovered. For now, let's review the types of objects in the Solar System. There are so many that we have divided them into the Sun and planets on this page, and everything else on the next one.

The Sun, a Star

The Sun, our star in the Solar System, plays a major role in life on Earth. The sun is about 300,000 times closer than the next closest star, Proxima Centauri. It takes light about 8.3 minutes to travel from the sun to Earth, while light from Proxima Centauri takes over four years. Size and temperature-wise, the sun is an average star, not unusually hot or cold, old or young, large or small. The Sun is an enormous ball about 1.4 million kilometers in diameter, with surface layers of incandescent gas and an interior temperature of millions of degrees. The sun goes through constant changes: energy output, brightness, size; all of which affect Earth.

As noted previously, the Sun contains over 99% of the mass in the Solar System. At roughly 1.4 million km in across, it is also the largest object in the Solar System. All of the other objects are significantly smaller than the Sun. Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\) is an image of the Sun collected by NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory. Near the center of the Sun is a large group of sunspots which are tiny, black spots on the Sun. This group contains over ten sunspots of varying sizes and is almost 128,000 kilometers across. The Earth could easily fit inside one of the smaller sunspots. The Sun is roughly spherical in shape and, in this image, is mostly light orange, with darker orange regions around the edges. There are four more tiny, lone spots scattered across the surface near the Sun's equator.

The Sun is the source of all visible light in the Solar System. For most of history, we have observed all the other objects by the sunlight reflected off their surface. The Sun also warms the atmosphere of Earth and provides energy for life on Earth through the process of photosynthesis.

Planets

There are eight planets in the Solar System, Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\) includes images of the eight planets and Earth's moon. The image was created by combining individual images of the planets. Note that the planets, along with Earth's moon are not to scale, that is, the relative size of each planet does not reflect its actual size compared to the others. In reality, Earth is much smaller than Jupiter, as opposed to being slightly smaller in this image. The main thing to notice is that the planets come in many different colors, from the blue of Uranus and Neptune to the orange of Mars, Saturn, and Jupiter. Mercury and the Moon are a similar gray color, and technically so is Venus, but its a special case that we will explain later. Saturn stands out for having large rings, while Earth is unique with its blue oceans and white clouds. The sections below will have images that compare the relative sizes of the planets.

Defining Planets

In the section about wandering stars, we reviewed how early astronomers noticed that certain objects moved in the sky relative to the pattern of fixed stars. The ancient Greeks called these objects wanderers or planetos in Greek, which later became the word planet in English. The early list of planets included five planets, Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn. Uranus, Neptune, and Pluto were discovered over time as telescopes became more powerful.

In the 1990s, telescope technology had made great advances and astronomers began to find objects of small size and icy composition similar to Pluto. In 2005, a team of astronomers found an object, that would eventually be named Eris, that was estimated to be larger than Pluto. Due to this and other discoveries, the International Astronomical Union (IAU), an astronomical society that approves the naming of astronomical objects, decided to change the definition of a planet. They also proposed a new term, dwarf planet, for Pluto and other objects of similar size. The IAU definition announced in 2006 is:

A planet in the Solar System is a celestial body that:

  1. is in orbit around the Sun,
  2. has sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape, and
  3. has cleared the neighborhood around its orbit.

While this original definition only defines planets orbitting the Sun, later documents expand the definition to an object orbiting a star that is not a star itself. The last part of the definition excludes any object that is in one of the Solar System's belts. This separates objects with different histories: planets formed by absorbing or knocking away any objects in its path, while dwarf planets formed in or spend most of their existence in orbits similar to a lot of other objects. For example, Pluto and it's neighbors are members of the Kuiper Belt. At the time, the IAU defined three dwarf planets: Pluto, Eris, and Ceres.

This definition also requires that planets must be round, which may seem like a strange requirement. This part of the definition separates planets from most smaller objects, such as asteroids and comets that can come in a variety of non-spherical shapes. These larger objects have a spherical shape because they have differentiated. At some point during formation, they were warm enough to be mostly liquid, the densest material sank to the center and the lightest materials floated to the surface. This is what the IAU means by hydrostatic equilibrium. It separates planets by size. There is no specific size limit because it depends on the composition of the object. In general, objects smaller than Pluto are not able to reach hydrostatic equilibrium. Objects that have differentiated may also have geologically active surfaces.

During the process of heating and differentiation, the material in the planets go through fundamental changes that erases their original state, similar to combining eggs, flour, sugar, and chocolate to bake a cake. When presented with a cake, the consumer does not know the exact ingredients. Smaller objects, such as asteroids and comets, that have not differentiated can maintain some of their original, primitive material. The shape of an object separates out planets from smaller objects that may contain material that has not changed since the formation of the Solar System. The dwarf planets are in between the planets and smaller objects because their round shape indicates that they are at least partially differentiated.

The announcement of this new definition was met with a very strong reaction from the astronomers and the public. Within the scientific community reactions ranged from satisfaction to rage. There was also complete indifference, because astronomers that study the Solar System are a small fraction of the astronomy population. Some astronomers objected to the short timeline and lack of broad discussion within the community. Members of the public were very upset about Pluto no longer being a planet. This was a hard change for people that had grown up memorizing the names of nine planets. The continuing debate indicates the challenges of defining categories in science.

When creating scientific categories, it is important to remember two things: 1. Any category should represent something significant about the properties of an object that separates it from other categories and 2. Reality is not a collection of categories. Astronomers categorize objects to learn more about the universe, not because the categories reflect a fixed truth. New information often means redefining catergories, including changing Pluto from a planet to a dwarf planet.

A dwarf planet has a roughly round shape and orbits the Sun, but does not clear its neighborhood. The IAU has officially designated Pluto, Eris, Haumea, Makemake, and Ceres as dwarf planets. There are many candidates dwarf planets. Some examples are Gonggong, Quaoar, Sedna, and Orcus. Ceres is an asteroid while the rest are Trans-Neptunian Objects. To confirm the candidates as dwarf planets, astronomers will need to confirm that they are round.

Properties of Planets

To learn more about the planets, it is helpful to study their properties. The first property is the planets average distance from the Sun. Earth is about 150 million kilometers from the Sun, approximately 400 times farther than the Moon. We call the average Earth-Sun distance an astronomical unit (AU). This unit is helpful because it is measured relative to the Earth. Planets that are closer to the Sun than Earth have values of less than 1, while the rest of the planets are at distance grater than 1.

A planet's orbital period is the time it takes a planet to travel once around the Sun. The orbital period of the Earth is 1 year. We could define each planet's orbital period to be its own year. The orbital period of Mars would be 1 Mars year. That would make it hard to compare values between planets, so we measure all of them in Earth years. Again, planets that are closer to the Sun than Earth have orbital periods of less than 1, and the rest have orbital periods that are greater than 1.

The diameter of a circle is a line that passes through the center of the circle and ends on the edge of the circle, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). In math, the size of a circle is measured by its diameter. They can also be measured by radius, the distance from the center to the edge of the circle or half of the diameter. The size of a spherical object, like a ball or star, is also measured by its diameter.

The volume of an object is a measurement of the physical space it occupies. Volume is measured in cubic units, such as cubic centimeters (cm3) or liters. Mass is a measurement of the amount of matter within an object. Matter is what the universe is made out of, including stars, planets, and humans.

A penny and an inflated balloon may both have the same mass, but they have very different volumes. The reason is that they also have very different densities, the amount of mass per unit volume. Density is the mass divided by the volume. The units of density are grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm3). Table \(\PageIndex{2}\) lists the densities of some familiar materials. The materials in the table with the highest densities are gold and lead with values of 19.3 and 11.3 g/cm3, respectively. The lowest density material is silica gel at 0.02 g/cm3. The value to remember is the density of liquid water: g/cm3. If an object has a density higher than one, then it will sink in a container of water, while objects with densities less than one will float.

Comparative Planetology

Comparative planetology is when astronomers study the planets by comparing their properties, such as those listed in Table \(\PageIndex{3}\). Remember that the distance from the Sun is measured in astronomical units (AU) and the orbital period is measured in Earth years. As noted previously, the orbital periods of Mercury and Venus are 0.24 and 0.62 year, both of which are less than one Earth year. The diameter, or the size, of the planets is measured in kilometers. 1 kilometer is equal to 0.62 miles.

The mass of the planets is given as a ratio to Earth's mass of 5.97 x 1024 kg. The value for each planet is calculated by taking the mass of that planet and dividing it by the mass of Earth. Values that are less than one mean that the planet has a lower mass than Earth and values that are greater than one are planets that are more massive than Earth. Mercury, Venus and Mars all have masses smaller than Earth's, while the rest of the planets are all much more massive than Earth. The last column of the table is density in units of g/cm3. Mercury has the highest density while Saturn has the lowest density, with values of 5.4 and 0.7 g/cm3, respectively. If there was a large enough container, Saturn would float in water.

Reading the table from top to bottom, or closest planet to the Sun to furthest, there is a sharp change in values between Mars and Jupiter. The planets suddenly become significantly larger, more massive, and much lower density. Astronomers use all of these properties to split the planets into two groups. Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are the inner planets, while Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune are the outer planets.

The Inner Planets

Figure \(\PageIndex{5}\) includes Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars, from left to right. The planets are the correct size relative to each other and in "true color" which means that the images are how a human would see them with their eyes. Each inner planet has a unique appearance compared to the others. Mercury is overall grey in color, with a few bright white spots on its surface surrounded by regions of light to medium-dark grey. There are no visible clouds on Mercury. Venus is also grey, but we are not looking at the surface. Venus has an atmosphere that covers the entire planet in a thick layer of clouds, making it impossible to see the surface from space. Unlike Jupiter and Saturn, Venus's atmosphere has no visible storms or color variations. The image of Earth depicts many white clouds, blue oceans, and a tan colored continent of Africa. Mars is mostly dark orange in color, with some regions that are dark brown and black. There are white, wispy clouds near both poles and a little bit of white ice on Mars' south pole.

When astronomers study a planet, they consider the overall composition of the entire planet. From this point of view, the Earth is mostly made of rock and metal with an extremely thin layer of lower density rock and water on the surface. Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars are also called the terrestrial planets because their overall composition is mostly rock and metal. The word terrestrial comes from the Latin word terra, which means both the entire planet Earth and the rocks on the surface of the Earth. In these planets, the most abundant rocks, called silicates, are made of silicon and oxygen, and the most common metal is iron. The density of Earth is 5.5 g/cm3, in between the values for rock and iron.

The Outer Planets

Figure \(\PageIndex{6}\) includes, from left to right, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Similar to Venus all four planets are covered in a thick atmosphere. Unlike Venus, there is no rocky surface beneath the clouds. Jupiter is the largest of the outer planets. It and has dark orange and white horizontal stripes which are clouds in the atmosphere. These stripes have complex structures such as ripples along the boundaries or large white spots. The Great Red Spot, an oval shaped storm that has lasted more than 50 years, is on Jupiter's bottom right.

Saturn is orange with faint, thin stripes that range from dark brown to light orange. In the image the planet and rings are tipped downward to the left so that the stripes and rings extend diagonally up to right and down to left. The rings extend outward from Saturn in a disk with a gap between the planet and the innermost ring. Rings change in color from white to light brown. There is a large gap in the middle of the rings, and another smaller one near the outer edge. Uranus and Neptune are both much smaller than Jupiter and Saturn. Uranus is light blue and has no structures in its atmosphere. Neptune is slightly darker blue, with few small white spots.

Due to their difference in size and color, the outer planets are split into two groups. Jupiter and Saturn are often called gas giants or Jovian planets. Jupiter and Saturn, have nearly the same chemical makeup as the Sun. They are composed primarily of the two elements hydrogen and helium, with 75% of their mass being hydrogen and 25% helium. As we will learn in their chapters, a better name for Jupiter and Saturn is the liquid giants because the majority of their interior is liquid hydrogen.

Uranus and Neptune were less efficient at attracting hydrogen and helium gas, so they have much smaller atmospheres in proportion to their cores. Uranus and Neptune are sometimes called the ice giants. Under the force of gravity, the heavier elements sink, or differentiate, toward the inner parts of a liquid or gaseous planet. All of the outer planets, have cores composed of heavier rock, metal, and ice. All of the outer planets have many moons and rings, although only Saturn's rings are large enough to easily observe.
