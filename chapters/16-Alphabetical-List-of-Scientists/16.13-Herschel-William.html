William Herschel

William Herschel was a German musician who emigrated to England and took up astronomy in his spare time. In 1781, he accidentally discovered the seventh planet, Uranus. It happens that Uranus had been observed a century before, but it was recorded as a star. Herschel’s discovery showed that there could be planets in the Solar System too dim to be visible to the unaided eye, but ready to be discovered with a telescope if we just knew where to look. Herschel wanted to name the new planet Georgium Sidus (George’s star) after King George III of his adopted country. Instead, Uranus was named after a god from the Roman pantheon, like the other planets.

In 1785, William Herschel made the first important discovery about the architecture of the Milky Way Galaxy (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)). Using a large reflecting telescope that he had built, William and his sister Caroline counted stars in different directions of the sky. They found that most of the stars they could see lay in a flattened structure encircling the sky, and that the numbers of stars were about the same in any direction around this structure. Herschel therefore concluded that the stellar system to which the Sun belongs has the shape of a disk or wheel, and that the Sun must be near the hub of the wheel.

Herschel first discovered infrared in 1800 while trying to measure the temperatures of different colors of sunlight spread out into a spectrum. He noticed that when he accidentally positioned his thermometer beyond the reddest color, it still registered heating due to some invisible energy coming from the Sun. This was the first hint about the existence of the other, invisible bands of the electromagnetic spectrum, although it would take many decades for our full understanding to develop.

One well-known binary star is Castor, located in the constellation of Gemini. By 1804, Herschel had noted that the fainter component of Castor had slightly changed its position relative to the brighter component. Here was evidence that one star was moving around another. It was actually the first evidence that gravitational influences exist outside the Solar System. A binary star system in which both of the stars can be seen with a telescope is called a visual binary.
