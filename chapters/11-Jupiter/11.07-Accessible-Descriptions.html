Figure 11.1.1 Jupiter

A color image of the whole planet Jupiter against black space.

Image Elements

  • Jupiter fills the center of the image as a large, round disk.
  • Horizontal bands of clouds circle the planet. Light cream and pale blue-white bands alternate with darker tan, orange, and brown bands.
  • The edges between the bands are wavy and swirled, showing turbulent motion in the atmosphere.
  • The Great Red Spot, an oval orange-red storm, is in the lower right, below the bright band at the equator.
  • A small, round black dot on the left side, below the equator, is the shadow of the moon Europa on the clouds.
  • A row of small white oval storms runs across the southern part of the planet, below the Great Red Spot.

Figure 11.1.2 Outer Planets Interiors

A diagram comparing the internal layers of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, drawn to scale.

Diagram Elements

  • Each planet is drawn as a cone shape, like a wedge cut from the planet. The point of the cone at the bottom is the center of the planet, and the wide top is the outer surface.
  • Each cone is divided into layers. Numbers along the left side give the radius at the top of each layer, in thousands of kilometers.
  • The layers are color coded: rock is tan, ice is light blue, metallic hydrogen is pink, and molecular hydrogen is gray.
  • From left to right, the cones are Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Jupiter is the largest and Neptune is the smallest.

Layers of Each Planet

  • Jupiter
    • Rock from the center to 7
    • Ice from 7 to 14
    • Metallic hydrogen from 14 to 59, the thickest layer
    • Molecular hydrogen from 59 to the surface at 71
  • Saturn
    • Rock from the center to 8
    • Ice from 8 to 16
    • Metallic hydrogen from 16 to 30
    • Molecular hydrogen from 30 to the surface at 60, the thickest layer
  • Uranus
    • Rock from the center to 8
    • Ice from 8 to 18, the thickest layer
    • Molecular hydrogen from 18 to the surface at 26
    • No metallic hydrogen layer
  • Neptune
    • Rock from the center to 10
    • Ice from 10 to 20, the thickest layer
    • Molecular hydrogen from 20 to the surface at 25
    • No metallic hydrogen layer

Comparison

  • Jupiter and Saturn are mostly hydrogen, with large metallic hydrogen layers. Uranus and Neptune are much smaller and are made mostly of ice and rock, with a thinner outer layer of hydrogen.

Figure 11.1.3 Jupiter in Radio Waves

A false-color radio image of the region around Jupiter on a black background.

Image Elements

  • The glowing region is long and narrow, stretching diagonally from the upper left to the lower right of the image.
  • Colors show the strength of the radio waves. White is the strongest, followed by light blue, green, red, and dark blue at the faint outer edges.
  • Two bright white lobes are near each end of the long shape, one at the upper left and one at the lower right.
  • A light blue band runs between the two lobes along the center of the shape.
  • Several small, curved bumps extend from the sides of the shape.
  • The glow comes from charged particles trapped in Jupiter's magnetosphere, not from the planet itself. The planet is slightly smaller than the green oval in the center.

Figure 11.3.2 Outer Planet Atmospheres

Four charts side by side showing how temperature changes with altitude in the atmospheres of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, with the main cloud layers marked.

Chart Elements

  • All four charts share the same axes.
  • The vertical axis is labeled Altitude (km) and goes from -300 at the bottom to 200 at the top, in increments of 100. Zero is near the visible cloud tops.
  • The horizontal axis is labeled Temperature (K) and goes from 0 on the left to 300 on the right, in increments of 100.
  • The background of each chart changes from dark blue at the bottom to orange and red at the top.
  • A yellow curve shows the temperature at each altitude.
  • Cloud layers are drawn as small blue, white, or cream cloud shapes, labeled with their chemical formulas.

Temperature Curves

  • In all four charts, the temperature drops from deep in the atmosphere up to a minimum near an altitude of 0 km, then rises slightly at higher altitudes.
  • Jupiter: begins at 300 K at -100 km, reaches a minimum of about 110 K near 0 km, and ends near 160 K at 150 km.
  • Saturn: begins at 300 K at -300 km, reaches a minimum of about 90 K near 0 km, and ends near 140 K at 200 km.
  • Uranus: begins near 150 K at -150 km, reaches a minimum of about 55 K near 0 km, and ends near 100 K at 150 km.
  • Neptune: begins near 280 K at -300 km, reaches a minimum of about 55 K near 50 km, and stays near 60 K up to 200 km.

Cloud Layers

  • Jupiter: from top to bottom, NH3 near -20 km, NH4HS near -50 km, and H2O near -80 km. N2H4(?) is labeled near the top of the chart.
  • Saturn: from top to bottom, NH3 near -100 km, NH4HS near -160 km, and H2O near -250 km. P2H4(?) is labeled near the top of the chart.
  • Uranus: CH4 near -40 km and H2S? near -90 km. Hydrocarbon ices are labeled above 0 km.
  • Neptune: CH4 near -60 km and H2S? near -110 km. Hydrocarbon ices are labeled above 0 km.
  • On Saturn, the same types of clouds as Jupiter form deeper in the atmosphere. Uranus and Neptune are colder, and their upper clouds are made of methane, CH4.

Figure 11.3.3 Outer Planet Winds

Four charts side by side showing how wind speed changes with latitude on Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, each drawn over an image of the planet.

Chart Elements

  • The vertical axis is labeled Latitude and goes from -90° at the bottom, the south pole, to 90° at the top, the north pole, in increments of 30°. The equator is at 0°.
  • The horizontal axis is labeled Eastward Wind Speed (m/s). Positive speeds are winds blowing east, faster than the planet's interior rotates. Negative speeds are winds blowing more slowly than the interior rotates.
  • Each chart has a dashed vertical line at 0 m/s.
  • The wind speed is plotted as a red curve. The horizontal scale is different for each planet.

Wind Speed Curves

  • Jupiter
    • The horizontal axis goes from -200 to 200 m/s, with tick marks at -100, 0, and 100.
    • The curve zigzags back and forth many times between the poles, showing many narrow bands of wind blowing in opposite directions.
    • The fastest eastward wind is about 150 m/s near 25° north, and winds near the equator are about 100 m/s.
    • The strongest westward wind is about -100 m/s near 20° to 30° south.
  • Saturn
    • The horizontal axis goes from -500 to 500 m/s, with tick marks at -300, 0, and 300.
    • The curve has one very large eastward peak of about 450 m/s at the equator.
    • At higher latitudes, the curve zigzags between 0 and about 150 m/s.
  • Uranus
    • The horizontal axis goes from -400 to 400 m/s, with tick marks at -200, 0, and 200.
    • The curve is smooth. It is about -50 m/s at the equator and about 200 m/s near 60° north and 60° south.
  • Neptune
    • The horizontal axis goes from -600 to 600 m/s, with tick marks at -300, 0, and 300.
    • The curve is smooth, with strong westward winds of about -350 m/s at the equator and eastward winds of about 250 m/s near 70° north and 70° south.

Comparison

  • Jupiter and Saturn have eastward winds at the equator, while Uranus and Neptune have westward winds at the equator and eastward winds at high latitudes.

Figure 11.4.1 Callisto

Two panels showing the moon Callisto and close-ups of its surface.

Image (a)

  • On the left
  • The whole moon Callisto as a round disk on a black background.
  • The surface is dark brown and gray, covered with many small, bright white spots. The bright spots are craters, and the surface is heavily cratered everywhere.

Image (b)

  • On the right
  • Two gray, high-resolution close-up images of Callisto's surface, one above the other, each drawn at a slant as if looking at the ground from an angle.
  • A small image of the whole moon in the lower right corner has lines pointing to the spot where the close-ups were taken.
  • A scale bar below the close-ups is labeled 5 km.
  • The top close-up is covered with many bright, pointed spires of ice that cast long shadows.
  • The bottom close-up has fewer spires, with smooth, dark ground between them and a small crater near the center.
  • As the icy spires erode, dark dust collects in the low areas and the spires disappear.

Figure 11.4.2 Ganymede

Two panels showing the moon Ganymede and a close-up of its surface.

Image (a)

  • On the left
  • The whole moon Ganymede as a round disk on a black background, in enhanced color.
  • Large, dark brown and gray regions are separated by lighter tan regions crossed by many thin lines.
  • Bright white and pink spots, mostly near the top and bottom, are young craters.

Image (b)

  • On the right
  • A gray, high-resolution close-up of Nicholson Regio on Ganymede.
  • A wide band of parallel grooves and ridges runs from the left side of the image to the upper right.
  • The surface on both sides of the band is darker, rough, and dotted with small craters.
  • An old impact crater has been split and pulled apart where the grooves cut through it, so it is no longer round.

Figure 11.4.3 Evidence for an Ocean on Europa

Two close-up images of the icy surface of Europa.

Image (a)

  • On the left
  • An enhanced-color image of the Conamara Chaos region, 70 kilometers wide. The colors are pale blue, white, tan, and reddish brown.
  • The surface is broken into many flat blocks of ice of different sizes and shapes, each crossed by fine parallel lines.
  • The blocks are surrounded by rough, jumbled material, and many seem to have shifted or rotated, like sea ice floating on an ocean.

Image (b)

  • On the right
  • A gray, high-resolution image of Europa's icy surface.
  • The surface is wrinkled and crossed by many long ridges running in different directions.
  • Two wide, dark double ridges run from top to bottom and cross over the thinner ridges.
  • Where ridges cross, the younger ridge lies on top of the older one.

Figure 11.4.4 Double Ridge on Europa

A gray, high-resolution image of a 15-kilometer-wide region of Europa's icy surface.

Image Elements

  • The surface is covered with many long, straight and gently curved ridges running in different directions.
  • A prominent double ridge, two parallel ridges with a groove between them, runs from the top left to the bottom right.
  • The double ridge crosses over all the ridges below it, so it is the youngest feature.
  • Other ridges also cross over still older ridges, showing several layers of ridges formed at different times.

Figure 11.4.5 Two Sides of Io

Two color images of the moon Io side by side on a black background, showing its two opposite hemispheres.

Image Elements

  • Each image is a round disk of Io's surface.
  • The surface is mostly yellow and orange, with patches of white and pale green.
  • Many small dark spots are scattered across both sides. These are volcanic vents and lava flows.
  • On the left disk, a large dark-centered spot near the center is surrounded by a ring of lighter material.
  • The orange deposits are sulfur snow, and the white deposits are sulfur dioxide.
  • There are no impact craters, because volcanic activity keeps covering the surface with new material.

Figure 11.4.7 Volcanic Changes on Io

Three color images in a row showing the same region of Io at three different times. The colors are exaggerated.

Image Elements

  • Each image is labeled with its date: April 1997 on the left, September 1997 in the center, and July 1999 on the right.
  • In all three images, the surface is mostly yellow, with a large orange-red ring, the deposit from the volcano Pele, on the left side. A dark vent is in the center of the ring.
  • April 1997: the area to the upper right of the ring is yellow, with a few small dark spots.
  • September 1997: a large, round, dark gray patch about 400 kilometers across appears in the upper right of the ring. It is the deposit from an eruption at the volcano Pillan Patera.
  • July 1999: the dark gray patch is smaller and partly covered by orange-red material from Pele. A new, smaller dark spot with a yellow ring around it is to its right, from a small volcano that erupted after 1997.
  • The surface of Io changes in only a few years because of frequent volcanic eruptions.

Figure 11.4.9 Three Icy Moons

Three gray, high-resolution images side by side comparing the surfaces of Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto at the same scale.

Image Elements

  • Europa, on the left: the surface is covered with long, crisscrossing ridges and cracks, with very few craters.
  • Ganymede, in the center: a band of curved grooves and ridges crosses the image, and there are some small craters, mostly in the smoother area at the lower right.
  • Callisto, on the right: the surface is covered with many craters of all sizes, with a rough, hilly region in the middle.
  • The number of craters increases from Europa to Ganymede to Callisto, so Europa has the youngest surface and Callisto has the oldest.
