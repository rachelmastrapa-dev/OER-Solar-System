Science and Society - Lunar Pareidolia

What do you see when you look up at a full Moon? A face, a rabbit, or a toad maybe? These are all examples of different objects seen on the Moon, depending on the culture and region from which the Moon is seen.This phenomenon actually has a name, called lunar pareidolia, when humans tend to perceive different patterns or objects depicted on the lunar surface. This phenomenon is not specific to just the Moon, as pareidolia describes the human tendency to attach a meaningful interpretation to a wide variety of objects (such as shapes in the clouds, or Abraham Lincoln on a piece of toast).

Because the Moon is tidally locked to Earth, we always see the same side, despite the changing phases of its illumination from the Sun. The striking color differences between lunar maria and highlands also contributes to the possibility of seeing a pattern or shape, especially if viewing the Moon with the naked eyes.

Some cultures describe a particular shape based on stories or other prominent symbols of the Moon. Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) shows a few examples of these depictions. The top figure, called the Banished Man, comes from Europe and tells a story of a man collecting sticks and has been banished to the Moon.

The second shows Yutu the rabbit, coming from Chinese, Japanese, and Korean cultures. Sometimes also drawn with mortar and pestle, the rabbit is thought to be mixing an elixir of life (from Chinese culture) or rice cake or other medicine (from Japanese and Korean cultures). The bottom three depictions show two versions of a Man's face on the Moon and a woman on the Moon with a jewel centered on Tycho's crater on the lunar surface. While both interpretations come from Western culture, the Man on the Moon is a popular interpretation in the Northern Hemisphere specifically. However, the Maori people also depict a woman that is lying next to a native tree, as well as in Chinese mythology, the goddess Chang'e is said to live on the Moon. 

One scientific reason that some people see faces while others see animals or other objects is the orientation from which they view the Moon, depicted in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\). If viewing from the Southern Hemisphere, the darker-colored maria are seen along the bottom and right side instead of upper left.

With the invention of binoculars, telescopes and cameras, humans are now able to easily see the Moon with higher magnification and resolution than others in history. Therefore, it may take a little more imagination and time spent looking at the real moon with just your eyes to see the shapes that previous generations saw so clearly.
