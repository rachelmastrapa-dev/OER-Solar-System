Figure 7.1.1 Moon at Different Phases

Two black-and-white images of the Moon comparing a partially lit phase with the full phase.

Image (a)

  • On the left
  • The Moon in a gibbous phase, with the right side lit by the Sun and the left edge in shadow.
  • The terminator, the boundary between the lit and dark parts of the Moon, runs down the left side.
  • Along the terminator, shadows cast by crater walls and mountains make the craters stand out sharply, especially in the heavily cratered region at the lower left.
  • The contrast between the darker maria and the brighter highlands is low.

Image (b)

  • On the right
  • The full Moon, completely lit by the Sun.
  • There are no shadows, so individual craters are hard to see.
  • The dark gray maria, mostly in the upper half of the Moon, contrast strongly with the brighter highlands.
  • A bright crater near the bottom, Tycho, has bright streaks of ejecta, called rays, that stretch outward across the surface in all directions.

Figure 7.1.2 Scientist on the Moon

A color image of an astronaut standing next to a large boulder on the lunar surface.

Image Elements

  • The astronaut, geologist Harrison Schmitt, is at the lower left in a white spacesuit and backpack, collecting samples.
  • A huge, fractured boulder fills the center and right side of the image. It is much taller and wider than the astronaut.
  • The ground is covered in gray dust with scattered small rocks.
  • Low, rounded gray hills form the horizon in the background.
  • The sky above the horizon is completely black, with no stars.

Figure 7.2.1 Two Sides of the Moon

Two gray images of the Moon side by side, comparing the hemisphere that faces Earth with the hemisphere that faces away from Earth.

Left Image

  • On the left
  • The near side of the Moon, the hemisphere that always faces Earth.
  • Large, smooth, dark gray regions, the maria, cover much of the upper and central parts of the disk.
  • Brighter, cratered highlands surround the maria, especially near the bottom.

Right Image

  • On the right
  • The far side of the Moon, the hemisphere that always faces away from Earth.
  • The surface is almost entirely bright, heavily cratered highlands, with craters of many sizes overlapping one another.
  • There are only a few small dark patches, including one small, dark mare in the upper left.

Comparison

  • The near side has far more maria than the far side, and the far side is more heavily cratered.

Figure 7.2.2 Lunar Highlands

A color image looking across the lunar highlands from orbit, toward the horizon at the upper left.

Image Elements

  • The surface is gray and covered with overlapping craters of many sizes.
  • A large crater with terraced walls and a flat floor is in the upper center. Several smaller craters lie on its floor.
  • Below it, a smaller, deep crater is mostly filled with black shadow and has a bright, sunlit rim.
  • Long, dark shadows inside many craters show that sunlight is striking the surface at a low angle.
  • Black space fills the upper left corner above the curved horizon.

Figure 7.2.3 Lunar Mountain

A black-and-white image of a large lunar mountain seen from the surface of the Moon.

Image Elements

  • Mount Hadley rises across the left and center of the image, with a broad, rounded peak and gentle slopes.
  • The right side of the mountain slopes down into a dark shadow.
  • The flat, gray foreground is dusty and dotted with small craters and rocks.
  • The sky above the mountain is completely black.
  • Small crosshair marks across the image are reference marks from the camera.
  • The mountain has no sharp ridges or jagged peaks, unlike many mountains on Earth.

Figure 7.2.4 Lunar Maria

A black-and-white image of Mare Imbrium taken from lunar orbit, looking toward the horizon.

Image Elements

  • The foreground and middle of the image are a smooth, dark gray plain with relatively few craters.
  • A medium-sized crater with a bright rim is left of center, and many small craters are scattered across the plain.
  • Faint, lighter streaks of ejecta cross the plain, coming from the direction of the horizon.
  • Near the curved horizon at the top of the image is the large crater Copernicus, surrounded by rough, bright, hilly terrain.
  • The sky above the horizon is black.
  • The mare is much less cratered than the highlands, which shows that its surface is younger.

Figure 7.2.5 Rock from a Lunar Mare

A color image of a single lunar rock sample on a white background.

Image Elements

  • The rock is light gray and speckled, with a rough, grainy surface.
  • Many small, dark holes are scattered across the surface, giving the rock a spongy appearance.
  • The holes were left by gas bubbles, showing that the rock formed from lava that cooled and hardened.

Figure 7.2.6 Mare Orientale

A gray image of the Mare Orientale impact basin, viewed from directly above.

Image Elements

  • The basin is a set of rings centered in the image, resembling a bull's-eye.
  • At the center is a dark, smooth patch of lava, the mare itself.
  • Bright, rough rings of mountains with steep, terraced edges surround the dark center.
  • The outer ring is about 1000 kilometers in diameter, roughly the distance between New York City and Detroit, Michigan.
  • The surrounding terrain is heavily cratered highlands, with ridges and grooves extending outward from the basin.
  • Orientale is located on the edge of the Moon as seen from Earth.

Figure 7.2.7 Footprint on Moon Dust

A color image looking down at a single boot print in the lunar soil.

Image Elements

  • The edges of the print are sharp and well defined, and shadows fill the deeper parts of the print.

Figure 7.3.2 Formation of an Impact Crater

Four diagrams in a row showing the stages of impact crater formation. In each, the lunar surface is light blue gray at the bottom and space is black above.

Image (a)

  • First on the left
  • A dark blue, rocky projectile with a faint, glowing trail approaches the surface from the upper right.

Image (b)

  • Second from the left
  • The projectile strikes the surface. A bright yellow-white burst of light and fragments sprays upward.
  • Red curved lines, representing a shock wave, spread out in semicircles into the ground below the impact.

Image (c)

  • Third from the left
  • The explosion is larger, and many fragments of rock, the ejecta, are thrown high above the surface.
  • The red shock wave rings have spread much deeper and wider into the ground.
  • A cavity has opened in the surface where the projectile struck.

Image (d)

  • On the right
  • The final crater is a wide, shallow bowl with raised rims on both sides.
  • A darker blue layer of fallen material lines the crater floor and extends over the rims onto the surrounding surface, forming the ejecta blanket.

Figure 7.3.3 Typical Impact Crater

A black-and-white image of King Crater on the far side of the Moon, seen from above.

Image Elements

  • The crater is roughly circular and fills most of the image.
  • The inner walls step down toward the floor in a series of terraces, with dark shadows along the left inner wall.
  • The crater floor is relatively flat and rough.
  • A cluster of peaks rises near the middle of the floor.
  • The terrain surrounding the crater is rough and cratered.

Figure 7.3.4 Meteor Crater

A color aerial image of Meteor Crater in northern Arizona.

Image Elements

  • The nearly circular, bowl-shaped crater is right of center in the image.
  • The steep inner walls are tan and brown, and the rim is raised slightly above the surrounding plain.
  • The crater floor is small and flat compared to the width of the crater.
  • The surrounding landscape is flat, light tan and gray-green desert crossed by dirt roads.
  • Small buildings next to the rim at the lower left show how large the crater is.
  • The crater has the simple bowl shape typical of smaller impact craters, without the terraced walls or central peaks of large craters.

Figure 7.3.5 Cratering Rates over Time

A chart showing how the rate of crater formation on the Moon has changed over time.

Chart Elements

  • The vertical axis is labeled Rate of Formation of Impact Craters and has no numerical values.
  • The horizontal axis is labeled Time before Present (in Billions of Years). It goes from about 5 on the left to 0, the present, on the right, with tick marks every 1 billion years.
  • The cratering rate is plotted as a red curve.

Curve

  • The curve begins at the upper left, near 4.6 billion years ago, as a dashed red line with a question mark. It is labeled Hypothetical cratering maximum. The dashed line shows that the earliest cratering rate is uncertain.
  • The solid curve drops very steeply from about 4.5 to 4.2 billion years ago. This part is labeled Cratering of highlands.
  • Between about 4.2 and 3.8 billion years ago, the curve begins to level off. This part has two labels, Imbrium and Orientale impacts near 4.2 billion years ago, and Formation of maria near 4.0 to 3.8 billion years ago.
  • By about 3 billion years ago, the curve is flat and close to the bottom of the chart, and it stays nearly constant to the present.
  • The flat section near 1 billion years ago is labeled Recent craters (Tycho, Copernicus).
  • Most lunar craters formed early in the Moon's history, and the cratering rate has been low ever since.

Figure 7.5.1 Pareidolias in the Moon

Five rows, each with two identical images of the full Moon side by side. The left image in each row is the plain full Moon, and the right image has a blue outline drawn over it showing a shape that some cultures see in the Moon.

Full Moon

  • The Moon is pale yellowish white, with darker gray maria mostly in the upper half and on the left side.

Outlines

  • From top to bottom
  • Top row: the Banished Man, a small human figure in the upper right of the Moon, carrying a bundle of sticks.
  • Second row: Yutu the rabbit, a large rabbit covering most of the Moon, with long ears extending to the upper right.
  • Third row: the Man in the Moon, a large face with eyes, nose, and mouth traced along the edges of the maria.
  • Fourth row: a second version of the Man in the Moon, a simple face with two round eyes near the top, a small nose in the middle, and a curved mouth near the bottom.
  • Bottom row: a woman's face in profile in the upper right, with her hair piled on top of her head. Lines radiate from a point near the bottom of the Moon, representing a jewel centered on the crater Tycho.
  • The same pattern of light and dark regions can be interpreted as many different shapes.
