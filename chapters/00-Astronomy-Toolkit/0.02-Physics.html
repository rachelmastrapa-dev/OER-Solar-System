The Physical Laws of Nature

The word physics comes from the Greek word physika meaning the natural things. The physical universe is enormously complex in its detail. Over the centuries, the curiosity of the human race has led us collectively to explore and catalog a tremendous wealth of information. In the face of all these details, we have discovered that a surprisingly small and unified set of physical laws can explain what we observe. Physics is concerned with describing the interactions of energy, matter, space, and time, and it is especially interested in what fundamental mechanisms underlie every phenomenon.

Mechanics

Summarize laws of motion, familiar to anyone that interacts with the world - just using math for prediction.

Objects are in motion everywhere we look. When you are resting, your heart moves blood through your veins. And even in inanimate objects, there is continuous motion in the vibrations of atoms and molecules. An understanding of motion is key to understanding other concepts in physics.To measure and predict, we need to define what we are measuring and how it changes. Mechanics is the study of the motion of objects in nature.

Position

In order to describe the motion of an object, you must first be able to describe its position where it is at any particular time. More precisely, you need to specify its position relative to a convenient reference frame. Earth is often used as a reference frame. For example, a rocket launch would be described in terms of the position of the rocket with respect to the Earth as a whole. In other cases, we use reference frames that are not stationary but are in motion relative to the Earth. To describe the position of a person in an airplane, for example, we use the airplane, not the Earth, as the reference frame.

Displacement

If an object moves relative to a reference frame, then the object's position changes. This change in position is known as displacement. The SI unit for displacement is the meter (m), but sometimes kilometers or other units of length are used. Displacement is a vector because it has a direction as well as a magnitude. Vectors are represented graphically by arrows. An arrow used to represent a vector has a length proportional to the vector's magnitude. The larger the magnitude, the longer the length of the vector. The arrow points in the same direction as the vector.

Distance

Although displacement is described in terms of direction, distance is not. Distance is a scalar which is any quantity that has a magnitude, but no direction. It is defined to be the magnitude or size of displacement between two positions. Since distance has no direction it is always positive. Note that the distance between two positions is not the same as the distance traveled between them. Distance traveled is the total length of the path traveled between two positions. This means that distance traveled can be larger than the distance or magnitude of display. When traveling in a city or neighborhood, the distance traveled depends on the available paths and the need to go around buildings. Meanwhile, the distance is a straight line that passes through barriers and buildings. Distance traveled also includes repeated motions such as pacing back and forth where the person can end up in the same position where they started.

Time

In physics, time is change, or the interval over which change occurs. It is impossible to know that time has passed unless something changes. The amount of time is calibrated by comparison with a standard. The SI unit for time is the second, (s). We are usually interested in elapsed time for a particular motion, such as how long it takes a student to walk across a classroom to their seat. To find elapsed time, we note the time at the beginning and end of the motion and subtract the two. Elapsed time is the difference between the ending time and beginning time. For example, a lecture may start at 11:00 A.M. and end at 11:50 A.M., so that the elapsed time would be 50 min.

Velocity

In everyday language, most people use the terms speed and velocity interchangeably. In physics, however, they do not have the same meaning and are distinct concepts. One major difference is that speed has no direction. Thus speed is a scalar. Velocity is displacement (change in position) divided by the time of travel. Since displacement is a vector, velocity is also a vector. The magnitude of velocity is speed. The SI unit for speed is meters per second (m/s), but you can use any kind of length over time such as miles per hour (mph).

Instantaneous speed is the magnitude of instantaneous velocity. For example, suppose the airplane passenger at one instant had an instantaneous velocity of -3.0 m/s (the minus meaning toward the rear of the plane). At that same time his instantaneous speed was 3.0 m/s. Or suppose that at one time during a shopping trip your instantaneous velocity is 40 km/h due north. Your instantaneous speed at that instant would be 40 km/h the same magnitude but without a direction. Average speed, however, is very different from average velocity.Average speed is the distance traveled divided by elapsed time.

In Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\), our trip is shown in velocity and time. We start out from home at time zero and travel at velocity of 12 km/hr for 15 minutes away from home, towards the store. The direction of travel is represented by the velocity being a positive number. When we turn around and travel back home, this is represented by the change from positive to negative 12 km/hr. The negative sign represents traveling in the opposite direction that the first part of the trip. Again, we did not make an instantaneous change in velocity. This chart is a simplification in reality. In astronomy we use negative and positive velocities to describe when an object is coming toward us (negative) or moving away (positive).

Acceleration

In everyday conversation, to accelerate means to speed up. The accelerator in a car can in fact cause it to speed up. The formal definition of acceleration is consistent with these notions, but more inclusive. Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes over time. Since velocity is a vector, acceleration is also a vector. This means that, in physics, acceleration is speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction. The SI units for acceleration are m/s2, meters per second squared or meters per second per second, which literally means by how many meters per second the velocity changes every second.

Newton's Laws of Motion

As a young man in college, Isaac Newton became interested in the motion of objects in the natural world. He eventually published his work in a book called Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica, often referred to as the Principia. At the beginning of the Principia, Newton proposes three laws that govern the motions of all objects

Newtons' First Law

Experience suggests that an object at is not moving, or is at rest, will remain unmoving unless something happens to it. Objects in motion tends to slow down and stop unless some effort is made to keep it moving. What Newton's first law of motion states, however, is that a body at rest remains at rest, or, if in motion, remains in motion at a constant velocity unless acted on by an external force. Rather than contradicting our experience, Newton's first law of motion states that there must be a cause, such as an external force, for there to be any change in velocity. Remember that a change in velocity can be a chang in magnitude or direction. An object sliding across a table or floor slows down due to the friction acting on the object. If there were no friction, Newton's first law states that the object would keep sliding forever unless an external force stopped it. Force is defined in the next section, but we are generally familiar with having to push something to make it move.

Momentum

Newton's first law is sometimes called the law of inertia, where inertia is the tendency of objects to keep doing what they are already doing. In other words, a stationary object stays put, and a moving object keeps moving unless some force intervenes. Newton's first law is a restatement of conservation of momentum. The law states that in the absence of any outside influence, there is a measure of an object's motion, called its momentum, that remains unchanged.

Momentum depends on three factors: how fast an object moves, (2) the direction of its motion, and (3) the mass of the object. Scientists use the term velocity to describe the speed and direction of motion. Momentum then can be defined as an object's mass times its velocity. The equation for momentum is p = mv where p is momentum, m is mass, and v is velocity. If an objects velocity is 0, then its momentum is 0. That value will stay the same unless something acts on the object m. This means that momentum is conserved or stays the same.

Newton's Second Law

Newton's second law states that the change of motion of an object is proportional to and in the direction of the force acting on it. In the sections on velocity and acceleration, we learned that velocity is the speed and direction of a moving object and acceleration is the change ing velocity. Newton's first law says that an external force causes acceleration.

Force

Our intuitive definition of force is a push or a pull on an object. We know that a push or pull has both magnitude and direction, so it is a vector. Both the magnitude and direction of a force can vary by large amounts. For example, a cannon exerts a strong force on a cannonball that is launched into the air. In contrast, Earth exerts only a tiny downward pull on a flea.

In equation form, Newton's second law of motion is

\[ F=ma, \nonumber \]

Where F is force, m is the mass, and a is acceleration. The SI unit for force is kg m/s2 which is also called a Newton (N).

Newton's Third Law

Newton's third law states that whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the first object experiences a force that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the force that it exerts. This law represents a certain symmetry in nature: Forces always occur in pairs, and one object cannot exert a force on another without experiencing a force itself. We sometimes refer to this law as action-reaction, where the force exerted is the action and the force experienced as a consequence is the reaction. This also menas that when two objects in a system interact with each other, it does not change the total momentum of the system. In the absence of an external force, the total momentum of the system is constant, or conserved. Some examples of Newton's third law is a rocket blasting off the surface of the Earth, or stubbing a toe on a chair. It is also demonstrated in the recoil that occurs when firing a rifle or cannon.

Summary of Newton's Laws

  • Newton's first law: Every object will continue to be in a state of rest or move at a constant speed in a straight line unless it is compelled to change by an outside force.
  • Newton's second law: The change of motion of an object is proportional to and in the direction of the force acting on it.
  • Newton's third law: For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction or: the mutual actions of two objects upon each other are always equal and act in opposite directions.

Circular Motion

Up to this point, we have discussed motion in straight lines. However, many objects in astronomy rotate or travel in a roughly circular path. The physics for circular motion and can be a little more complicated, so we will highlight a couple important properties that will be important in this textbook.

Centripetal Acceleration

We defined acceleration as a change in velocity, either in its magnitude or in its direction, or both. When an object moves along a circular path, the direction of its velocity changes constantly, so there is always an associated acceleration, even if the speed of the object is constant. You experience this acceleration yourself when you turn a corner in your car. What you notice is a sideways acceleration because you and the car are changing direction. The sharper the curve and the greater your speed, the more noticeable this acceleration will become. In this section we briefly examine the direction and magnitude of that acceleration.

Imagine an object moving in a circular path at constant speed, called uniform circular motion. Acceleration is in the direction of the change in velocity, which points directly toward the center of rotation, the center of the circular path. Centripetal acceleration is the acceleration of an object moving in uniform circular motion. Centripetal means toward the center or center seeking. The equation for centripetal acceleration is:

\[a_c=\frac{v^{2}}{r} \nonumber \]

where ac is the centripetal acceleration, r is the radius of the circle, and v is the velocity. Centripetal acceleration is greater at high speeds and smaller radius. Compare this to driving a car or riding a bike. It can be very challenging to take a sharp turn at high speed.

Angular Momentum

Why does Earth keep on spinning? What started it spinning to begin with? And how does an ice skater manage to spin faster simply by pulling her arms in? Why does she not have to push herself to spin faster? Questions like these have answers based in angular momentum, the rotational analog to linear momentum. The equation for angular momentum is similar to the equation for momentum p = mv:

\[L=I \omega. \nonumber \]

where I is the moment of inertia and ω is the angular speed. The moment of inertia represents how hard it is to change an objects rotation rate, while the angular speed is measured in units of angles instead of length. The equation for moment of inertia depends on the shape of the rotating object. The moment of inertia of a sphere is:

[I_s=\frac{2}{5}mr^2 \nonumber \]

where Is is the moment of inertia of a sphere, m is the mass of the sphere, and r is the radius of the sphere. Since Earth has a large radius and mass, then its moment of moment of interia is going to be very large. Since angular momentum is proportional to moment of inertia, that means the angular momentum of the Earth is very large, even if it is rotating very slowly, which it is not.

Just like linear momentum, angular momentum is also conserved. This means that a rotating object will continue at a constant rate unless another object exerts a force on it. Let's look at the example of an ice skater to see how angular momentum is conserved. The moment of inertia of a person is difficult to calculate, but like a sphere, it is proportional to radius. The skater starts her spin by kicking off of the ice with her arms out. The length of her arm represents her radius. If she pulls her arms into her body, she reduces her radius, which in turn reduces her moment of inertia. Since angular momentum is conserved, the value for L needs to stay the same. With a lower moment of inertia, the angular speed needs to increase to compensate. This is how ice skaters take advantage of angular momentum to spin quickly.

Angular momentum also applies to objects traveling in a circular path, such as a planet orbiting the Sun. In astronomy we will be studying objects traveling in circular paths, and rotating objects. An astronomy example of the conservation of angular momentum is a shrinking cloud of dust or a star collapsing on itself. As material moves to a lesser distance from the spin center, the speed of the material increases to conserve angular momentum.

Gravity

Newton's laws of motion show that objects at rest will stay at rest and those in motion will continue moving uniformly in a straight line unless acted upon by a force. Thus, it is the straight line that defines the most natural state of motion. We can see where the force is coming from when a foot kicks a ball, but what force is acting when we drop our phone. Newton proposed that every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force that he called gravity. In this model, our phone falls from our hand because of the gravitational force of the Earth acting on the phone. The gravitational force is always attractive, and it depends only on the masses involved and the distance between them. Stated in modern language, Newton's universal law of gravitation states that every particle in the universe attracts every other particle with a force along a line joining them. The force is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The relationship is stated as:

\[F_g= \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^{2}} \nonumber \]

where Fg is the gravitational force, G is the gravitational constant: 6.67 x 10-11 N-m2/kg2, m1 is the first object's mass, m2 is the second object's mass, and r is the distance between the two objects. In this equation, the gravitational force is directly proportional to the mass of the two objects, so larger objects have a larger force of gravity. The gravitational force is inversely proportional to the distance between the objects. This is called an inverse square relationship, where the greater the distance between the two objects, the smaller the force between these two objects - squared. If the first distance was 1 meter and the second distance was two meters, the variation in the force would be 1/4 of the original force at the second distance. One thing to keep in mind is that the force of gravity gets weaker with distance, but it never goes to zero. No matter how far away two objects are from each other, there is some amount of gravitational force between them, but it is very, very weak.

The gravitational constant, G, was derived emperically by Newton. This means that he made repeated measurements of objects of varying masses at a range of distances. He was able to discover the proportionality relationships between the variables, but just plugging in values did not produce the correct number. The equation needed a constant to produce the correct answers that fit the data. The result was the gravitational constant, 6.67 x 10-11 N-m2/kg2. This means that he could then put in values for distance in meters and the mass of the two objects in kilograms, and he could calculate the force in Newtons. With the gravitational constant we do not have to directly measure the force of gravity between every two objects. Intead, we can plug in the masses and distances of any objects in the universe and calculate their force of gravity. Gravity is a universal property of matter because it can be applied to all matter in the universe.

One thing to keep in mind is that the gravitational constant, G, is a very small number. This means that for small masses, the force of gravity is very small. For example, two 1.000 kg masses separated by 1.000 m will experience a gravitational attraction of 6.673 x 10-11 N. This is an extraordinarily small force, but it makes sense because we do not see small objects moving toward each other. We often think of gravity as a strong force because of how glasses shatter when they fall, or how much it hurts to fall down, but it takes something as massive as Earth to exert that kind of force. Meanwhile, we are unaware that even large objects like mountains exert gravitational forces on us because we do not sense ourselves being pulled toward the mountain. Fortunately, scientists can build machines that can detect these changes in gravitationally and they are used regularly by geologists to understand the structures of mountains and the crust of Earth.

One limitation of the equation for the gravitational forces is that it does not include the size or shape of the objects. Instead, acts as if the entire mass of each object is concentrated at one specific point called the center of mass. Reality is more complicated than that, and there are more complicated versions of this equation that take into account the size and shape of objects. This is an extremely useful tool in astronomy, since we are often dealing with very large objects.

The concept of universal gravitation also predicts that, the force of gravity is the same on all objects on the surface of Earth, and therefore they should all fall at the same rate. We often think that small items should fall more slowly than heavy items. This idea is amplified by the existence of air resistance, which will make a piece of paper fall more slowly than a pencil. However, if we can eliminate air resistance and friction, then in a given location all objects fall toward the center of Earth with the same constant acceleration, independent of their mass, a concept predicted by Galileo. There are two videos with experiments proving this in the Further Exploration box at the end of this page. When something is falling, it is accelerating because the father it falls, the faster it is falling. The acceleration of gravity on the surface of Earth, g, is 9.80 m/s2.

Keep in mind that the idea of an invisible force that causes all objects to attract to each other was a highly controversial, especially since Newton could not say why matter attracts each other. Since then, Newton's universal law of gravitation was able to predict the presence of, and led to the discovery of the planet Neptune, demonstrating that gravity does apply to all matter. There have been some changes to the law of gravity, including Albert Einstein's change to matter causing curvurture of spactime, a concept we will not get into here. Scientists still do not know exactly how matter attracts each other, however, the models and equations that we use to simulate not only agree with what we are able to measure, but also predict the presence of objects and material that are otherwise undetectable.

Weight

Mass and weight are often used interchangeably in everyday language. However, in science, these terms are distinctly different from one another. Mass is a measure of how much matter is in an object. The typical measure of mass is the kilogram. Weight, is a measure of the force of gravity acting on an object. Weight is equal to the mass of an object, m, multiplied by the acceleration due to gravity, g. Like any other force, weight is measured in terms of Newtons.

When an object is dropped, it accelerates toward the center of Earth. Newton's second law states that a net force on an object is responsible for its acceleration. If air resistance is negligible, the net force on a falling object is the gravitational force, commonly called its weight, w. Weight is a vector because it has a direction: down. Since the equation for force is F = ma, which is a mass times acceleration, then the equation for weight is w = mg, where m is the mass of the object and g is the acceleration of gravity on Earth.

Assuming the mass of an object is kept intact, it will remain the same, regardless of its location. However, because weight depends on the acceleration due to gravity, the weight of an object can change when the object enters into a region with stronger or weaker gravity. For example, the acceleration due to gravity on the Moon is \(1.67 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}\) (which is much less than the acceleration due to gravity on Earth, \(9.80 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}\)). If you measured your weight on Earth and then measured your weight on the Moon, you would find that you weigh much less, even though you do not look any skinnier. This is because the force of gravity is weaker on the Moon. In fact, when people say that they are losing weight, they really mean that they are losing mass (which in turn causes them to weigh less).

The broadest definition of weight in this sense is that the weight of an object is the gravitational force on it from the nearest large body, such as Earth, the Moon, the Sun, and so on. This is the most common and useful definition of weight in physics. It differs dramatically, however, from the definition of weight used by NASA and the popular media in relation to space travel and exploration. When they speak of weightlessness and microgravity, they are really referring to the phenomenon we call free-fall in physics. We shall use the above definition of weight, and we will make careful distinctions between free-fall and actual weightlessness. Keep in mind that when a spacecraft is orbitting Earth, it is technically falling towards Earth, but missing. This means that the astronauts in orbit are in a constant state of falling: free-fall.

Energy

Energy is one of the most fundamental and universal concepts of physical science, but one that is remarkably difficult to define in way that is meaningful. This perhaps reflects the fact that energy is not a thing that exists by itself, but is rather an attribute of matter and light that can manifest itself in various ways. It is defined by what is able to do, which is to make a change to a system. It can be observed and measured only indirectly through its effects on matter that acquires, loses, or possesses it. Energy can take many forms is conserved. It can be passed from one object or place to another, but it can never disappear.

Types of Energy

There are many types of energy, but they are all derived from two many categories: kinetic and potential. Kinetic energy is associated with the motion of an object. An object with a mass, m, and moving at a velocity, v, possesses the kinetic energy \( \frac{1}{2} mv^2\). This v-squared part is important because if you double speed, the energy is four times larger. Potential energy is energy an object dependent on its position. It might have energy due to its location in a gravitational, electrical, or magnetic field. Fields are area of influence of an object that has a property that affects its surroundings. All matter creates a gravitational field around it that is stronger near the object or farther away. For example, if an object of mass m is raised off the floor to a height h, its potential energy increases by mgh, where g is the acceleration of gravity.

Other types of energy either potential or kinetic energy. The sum total of all of this microscopic-scale randomized kinetic energy within an obkect is thermal energy. Although we cannot directly see thermal energy in action, we can certainly feel it because ot correlates directly with the temperature of an object. Nuclear energy, which is produced in nuclear power plants, is the potential energy stored inside the nucleus of specific isotopes of elements such as plutonium.

Energy Units

The basic unit of energy is the joule. One joule is the amount of energy needed to produce a a force of 1 newton acting over a distance of 1 m: 1 J = 1 N-m. One newton is the amount of force required to accelerate a 1 kg mass by 1 meter per second in one second: 1 m/s2, so the basic dimensions of the joule are kg m2/s2. A watt (W) is a unit of power, which is energy used or given off per unit time. It is measured in joules per second (J/s)

Other units of energy include the calorie and the BTU (British thermal unit). These are defined in terms of the heating effect on water. One calorie is the amount of energy it takes to heat 1 gram of water by 1°C at 1 atmosphere of pressure. You may have heard of the calorie in relation to the amount of energy stored in food. One thing to keep in mind is that the amount of energy in a single calorie is very small, the calories listed on food packaging are actually kilocalories or in units of 1000 calories. Another energy unit that is commonly employed in atomic physics is the electron volt. this is the kinetic energy that an electron acquires upon being accelerated across a 1-volt potential difference. One eV is 1.6022-19 J.

Conservation of Mass and Energy

Energy, as we have noted, is conserved, making it one of the most important physical quantities in nature. The law of conservation of energy states: total energy is constant in any process. It may change in form or be transferred from one system to another, but the total remains the same. Einstein demonstrated that energy and mass are interchangeable by his famous equation E = mc2. If energy is conserved and energy and mass are interchangeable than mass is also conserved. Energy is conserved by changing into a different type of energy or by changing into matter and mass is conserved by matter changing into a different form or converting into energy. For now, let's consider the ways that energy can transform into other forms of energy.

Transformation of Energy

Pick up a book and hold it above a table top. You have just increased its potential energy in Earth's gravitational field. Now let it drop. Its newly-acquired potential energy changes to kinetic energy as it accelerates downward at a velocity increasing by 9.8 m/sec every second. What happens to that kinetic energy after the book stops moving? It is still there, but you can no longer see its effect. It has now become dispersed as thermal kinetic energy, or heat, into the molecules of the book, the table top, and, ultimately, into the surroundings, including the air. If, instead, you dropped something made of glass, some of that energy would have gone into shattering the object.

The transformation of energy from one form into others is happening all the time. The chemical energy in food is converted into thermal energy through metabolism. The energy in light is converted into chemical energy through photosynthesis. In a larger example, the chemical energy contained in coal is converted into thermal energy as it burns to turn water into steam in a boiler. This thermal energy in the steam in turn is converted to mechanical energy as it spins a turbine, which is connected to a generator to produce electrical energy.Heat and work are terms in physics for transformation of energy.

Heat

When a warmer body is brought into contact with a cooler body, thermal energy flows from the warmer one to the cooler until their two temperatures are identical. The warmer body loses a quantity of thermal energy, and the cooler body acquires the same amount of energy. We describe this process by saying that an amount of energy in joules of heat has passed from the warmer body to the cooler one. It is important, however, to understand that heat is the transfer of energy due to a difference in temperature.

There are three mechanisms by which heat can be transferred: conduction, radiation, and convection. In Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\) a boiling kettle of water is demonstrating all three types of heat transfer. Conduction is occurring at the point where the flames are in contact with the kettle. The heat from the flames is transferring into the bottom of the kettle. Conduction is also the movement of heat through an object. When the kettle was first placed above the fire, the bottom of the kettle was hot, and the top was room temperature. As time passed, the heat was conducted from bottom up through its sides to the top. Convection is occuring in the boiling water. The heat from the bottom of the kettle heats the water at the bottom of the kettle. The hot water rises to the top of the water, and cooler water sinks to the bottom of the kettle, where it is heated, repeating the cycle into the water eventually evaporates. Radiation is the release of heat from an object. The warm kettle is releasing heat into the air around it, warming the air.

Work

Work is the transfer of energy by any process other than heat. Mechanical work is done when a force F displaces an object by a distance d: W = Fd in units of N-m. Work, like energy, can take various forms: mechanical, electrical, gravitational, etc. Performance of work involves a transformation of energy. When a book drops to the floor, gravitational work is done, and the potential energy the book had before it was dropped is converted into kinetic energy which is ultimately dispersed as thermal energy.
