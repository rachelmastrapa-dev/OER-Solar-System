<h2>Gravity and Orbits</h2>

<p class="lt-phys-64660"><span data-type="footnote-ref-content">Generally</span><span data-type="footnote-ref-content"> we use standard metric (or SI) units in this book. The proper metric unit of density in that system is kg/m<sup>3</sup>. But to most people, g/cm<sup>3</sup> provides a more meaningful unit because the density of water is exactly 1 g/cm<sup>3</sup>, and this is useful information for comparison. Density expressed in g/cm<sup>3</sup> is sometimes called specific density or specific weight.</span></p>

<p class="lt-phys-64660">Newton’s laws of motion show that objects at rest will stay at rest and those in motion will continue moving uniformly in a straight line unless acted upon by a force. Thus, it is the</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">In Newton’s time, gravity was something associated with Earth alone. Everyday experience shows us that Earth exerts a gravitational force upon objects at its surface. If you drop something, it accelerates toward Earth as it falls. Newton’s insight was that Earth’s gravity might extend as far as the Moon and produce the force required to curve the Moon’s path from a straight line and keep it in its orbit. He further hypothesized that gravity is not limited to Earth, but that there is a general force of attraction between all material bodies. If so, the attractive force between the Sun and each of the planets could keep them in their orbits. (This may seem part of our everyday thinking today, but it was a remarkable insight in Newton’s time.)</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">Once <span class="no-emphasis" data-type="term" group-by="N">Newton</span> boldly hypothesized that there was a universal attraction among all bodies everywhere in space, he had to determine the exact nature of the attraction. The precise mathematical description of that gravitational force had to dictate that the planets move exactly as Kepler had described them to (as expressed in Kepler’s three laws). Also, that gravitational force had to predict the correct behavior of falling bodies on Earth, as observed by Galileo. How must the force of gravity depend on distance in order for these conditions to be met?</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">The answer to this question required mathematical tools that had not yet been developed, but this did not deter Isaac Newton, who invented what we today call calculus to deal with this problem. Eventually he was able to conclude that the magnitude of the force of gravity must decrease with increasing distance between the Sun and a planet (or between any two objects) in proportion to the inverse square of their separation. In other words, if a planet were twice as far from the Sun, the force would be <math display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="true">(</mo><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>2</mn></mfrac><msup><mo stretchy="true">)</mo><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><mo stretchy="true">(</mo><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>2</mn></mfrac><msup><mo stretchy="true">)</mo><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></annotation-xml></semantics></math>, or <math display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>4</mn></mfrac></mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>4</mn></mfrac></mrow></annotation-xml></semantics></math> as large. Put the planet three times farther away, and the force is <math display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="true">(</mo><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>3</mn></mfrac><msup><mo stretchy="true">)</mo><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><mo stretchy="true">(</mo><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>3</mn></mfrac><msup><mo stretchy="true">)</mo><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></annotation-xml></semantics></math>, or <math display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>9</mn></mfrac></mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>9</mn></mfrac></mrow></annotation-xml></semantics></math> as large.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">Newton also concluded that the gravitational attraction between two bodies must be proportional to their masses. The more mass an object has, the stronger the pull of its gravitational force. The gravitational attraction between any two objects is therefore given by one of the most famous equations in all of science:</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661"><math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>F</mi><mrow><mtext>gravity</mtext></mrow></msub><mo>=</mo></mrow></mrow><mrow><mrow><mi>G</mi><mfrac><mrow><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>2</mn></msub></mrow><mrow><msup><mi>R</mi><mrow><msub><mrow></mrow><mn>2</mn></msub></mrow></msup></mrow></mfrac></mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><msub><mi>F</mi><mrow><mtext>gravity</mtext></mrow></msub></mrow></annotation-xml><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><mo>=</mo><mi>G</mi><mfrac><mrow><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>2</mn></msub></mrow><mrow><msup><mi>R</mi><mrow><msub><mrow></mrow><mn>2</mn></msub></mrow></msup></mrow></mfrac></mrow></annotation-xml></semantics></math></p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">where <em data-effect="italics">F</em><sub>gravity</sub> is the gravitational force between two objects, <em data-effect="italics">M</em><sub>1</sub> and <em data-effect="italics">M</em><sub>2</sub> are the masses of the two objects, and <em data-effect="italics">R</em> is their separation. <em data-effect="italics">G</em> is a constant number known as the <em data-effect="italics">universal gravitational constant</em>, and the equation itself symbolically summarizes Newton’s <em data-effect="italics">universal law of gravitation</em>. With such a force and the laws of motion, Newton was able to show mathematically that the only orbits permitted were exactly those described by Kepler’s laws.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">Newton’s <span class="no-emphasis" data-type="term" group-by="u">universal law of gravitation</span> works for the planets, but is it really universal? The gravitational theory should also predict the observed acceleration of the Moon toward Earth as it orbits Earth, as well as of any object (say, an apple) dropped near Earth’s surface. The falling of an apple is something we can measure quite easily, but can we use it to predict the motions of the Moon?</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">Recall that according to Newton’s second law, forces cause acceleration. Newton’s universal law of gravitation says that the force acting upon (and therefore the acceleration of) an object toward Earth should be inversely proportional to the square of its distance from the center of Earth. Objects like apples at the surface of Earth, at a distance of one Earth-radius from the center of Earth, are observed to accelerate downward at 9.8 meters per second per second <math display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>9.8</mn><msup><mtext> m/s</mtext><mn>2</mn></msup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>9.8</mn><msup><mtext> m/s</mtext><mn>2</mn></msup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></annotation-xml></semantics></math>.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">It is this force of gravity on the surface of Earth that gives us our sense of <em data-effect="italics">weight.</em> Unlike your mass, which would remain the same on any planet or moon, your weight depends on the local force of gravity. So you would weigh less on Mars and the Moon than on Earth, even though there is no change in your mass. (Which means you would still have to go easy on the desserts in the college cafeteria when you got back!)</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">The Moon is 60 Earth radii away from the center of Earth. If gravity (and the acceleration it causes) gets weaker with distance squared, the acceleration the Moon experiences should be a lot less than for the apple. The acceleration should be <math display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><msup><mrow><mo stretchy="true">(</mo><mn>1</mn><mtext>/</mtext><mn>60</mn><mo stretchy="true">)</mo></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn><mtext>/</mtext><mn>3600</mn></mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><msup><mrow><mo stretchy="true">(</mo><mn>1</mn><mtext>/</mtext><mn>60</mn><mo stretchy="true">)</mo></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn><mtext>/</mtext><mn>3600</mn></mrow></annotation-xml></semantics></math> (or 3600 times less—about <math display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mn>0.00272</mn><msup><mtext> m/s</mtext><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><mn>0.00272</mn><msup><mtext> m/s</mtext><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></annotation-xml></semantics></math>). This is precisely the observed acceleration of the Moon in its orbit. (As we shall see, the Moon does not fall <em data-effect="italics">to</em> Earth with this acceleration, but falls <em data-effect="italics">around</em> Earth.) Imagine the thrill Newton must have felt to realize he had discovered, and verified, a law that holds for Earth, apples, the Moon, and, as far as he knew, everything in the universe.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">With eight times the volume, Earth’s radius would double. This means the gravitational force at the surface would reduce by a factor of <math display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow> <mo stretchy="true">(</mo><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>2</mn></mfrac><msup><mo stretchy="true">)</mo><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>4</mn></mfrac> </mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow> <mo stretchy="true">(</mo><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>2</mn></mfrac><msup><mo stretchy="true">)</mo><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>4</mn></mfrac> </mrow></annotation-xml></semantics></math>, so a person would weigh only one-fourth as much.Gravity is a “built-in” property of mass. Whenever there are masses in the universe, they will interact via the force of gravitational attraction. The more mass there is, the greater the force of attraction. Here on Earth, the largest concentration of mass is, of course, the planet we stand on, and its pull dominates the gravitational interactions we experience. But everything with mass attracts everything else with mass anywhere in the universe.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">Newton’s law also implies that gravity never becomes zero. It quickly gets weaker with distance, but it continues to act to some degree no matter how far away you get. The pull of the Sun is stronger at Mercury than at Pluto, but it can be felt far beyond Pluto, where astronomers have good evidence that it continuously makes enormous numbers of smaller icy bodies move around huge orbits. And the Sun’s gravitational pull joins with the pull of billions of others stars to create the gravitational pull of our Milky Way Galaxy. That force, in turn, can make other smaller galaxies orbit around the Milky Way, and so on.</p>

<p class="has-noteref lt-phys-64661">Why is it then, you may ask, that the astronauts aboard the Space Shuttle appear to have no gravitational forces acting on them when we see images on television of the astronauts and objects floating in the spacecraft? After all, the astronauts in the shuttle are only a few hundred kilometers above the surface of Earth, which is not a significant distance compared to the size of Earth, so gravity is certainly not a great deal weaker that much farther away. The astronauts feel “weightless” (meaning that they don’t feel the gravitational force acting on them) for the same reason that passengers in an elevator whose cable has broken or in an airplane whose engines no longer work feel weightless: they are falling (Figure 3.9).<sup data-type="footnote-number">2</sup>&nbsp;</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">When <em data-effect="italics">falling</em>, they are in free fall and accelerate at the same rate as everything around them, including their spacecraft or a camera with which they are taking photographs of Earth. When doing so, astronauts experience no additional forces and therefore feel “weightless.” Unlike the falling elevator passengers, however, the astronauts are falling <em data-effect="italics">around</em> Earth, not <em data-effect="italics">to</em> Earth; as a result they will continue to fall and are said to be “in orbit” around Earth (see the next section for more about orbits).</p>

<h3 class="lt-phys-64661">Orbital Motion and Mass</h3>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">Kepler’s laws describe the orbits of the objects whose motions are described by Newton’s laws of motion and the law of gravity. Knowing that gravity is the force that attracts planets toward the Sun, however, allowed Newton to rethink Kepler’s third law. Recall that Kepler had found a relationship between the orbital period of a planet’s revolution and its distance from the Sun. But Newton’s formulation introduces the additional factor of the masses of the Sun (<em data-effect="italics">M</em><sub>1</sub>) and the planet (<em data-effect="italics">M</em><sub>2</sub>), both expressed in units of the Sun’s mass. Newton’s universal law of gravitation can be used to show mathematically that this relationship is actually</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661"><math display="block"><semantics><mrow><mrow><msup><mi>a</mi><mn>3</mn></msup><mo>=</mo><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo>)</mo><mspace width="0.2em"></mspace></mrow></mrow><mrow><mrow><mo>×</mo><mspace width="0.2em"></mspace><msup><mi>P</mi><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><msup><mi>a</mi><mn>3</mn></msup></mrow></annotation-xml><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><mo>=</mo><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo>)</mo><mspace width="0.2em"></mspace><mo>×</mo><mspace width="0.2em"></mspace><msup><mi>P</mi><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></annotation-xml></semantics></math></p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">where <em data-effect="italics">a</em> is the semimajor axis and <em data-effect="italics">P</em> is the orbital period.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">How did Kepler miss this factor? In units of the Sun’s mass, the mass of the Sun is 1, and in units of the Sun’s mass, the mass of a typical planet is a negligibly small factor. This means that the sum of the Sun’s mass and a planet’s mass, <math display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo>)</mo></mrow></mrow><annotation-xml encoding="MathML-Content"><mrow><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><msub><mi>M</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo>)</mo></mrow></annotation-xml></semantics></math>, is very, very close to 1. This makes Newton’s formula appear almost the same as Kepler’s; the tiny mass of the planets compared to the Sun is the reason that Kepler did not realize that both masses had to be included in the calculation. There are many situations in astronomy, however, in which we <em data-effect="italics">do</em> need to include the two mass terms—for example, when two stars or two galaxies orbit each other.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">Including the mass term allows us to use this formula in a new way. If we can measure the motions (distances and orbital periods) of objects acting under their mutual gravity, then the formula will permit us to deduce their masses. For example, we can calculate the mass of the Sun by using the distances and orbital periods of the planets, or the mass of Jupiter by noting the motions of its moons.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64661">Indeed, Newton’s reformulation of Kepler’s third law is one of the most powerful concepts in astronomy. Our ability to deduce the masses of objects from their motions is key to understanding the nature and evolution of many astronomical bodies. We will use this law repeatedly throughout this text in calculations that range from the orbits of comets to the interactions of galaxies.&nbsp;</p>

<h3 class="lt-phys-64693">Geological Activity</h3>

<p class="lt-phys-64693">The crusts of all of the terrestrial planets, as well as of the larger moons, and Pluto, have been modified over their histories by both internal and external forces. Externally, each has been battered by a slow rain of projectiles from space, leaving their surfaces pockmarked by impact craters of all sizes (see Figure 7.4). We have good evidence that this bombardment was far greater in the early history of the solar system, but it certainly continues to this day, even if at a lower rate. The collision of more than 20 large pieces of <span class="no-emphasis" data-type="term" group-by="C">Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9</span> with Jupiter in the summer of 1994 (see Figure 7.13) is one dramatic example of this process.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64693">During the time all the planets have been subject to such impacts, internal forces on the terrestrial planets have buckled and twisted their crusts, built up mountain ranges, erupted as volcanoes, and generally reshaped the surfaces in what we call geological activity. (The prefix <em data-effect="italics">geo</em> means “Earth,” so this is a bit of an “Earth-chauvinist” term, but it is so widely used that we bow to tradition.) Among the terrestrial planets, Earth and Venus have experienced the most geological activity over their histories, although some of the moons in the outer solar system are also surprisingly active. In contrast, our own Moon is a dead world where geological activity ceased billions of years ago.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64693">Geological activity on a planet is the result of a hot interior. The forces of volcanism and mountain building are driven by heat escaping from the interiors of planets. As we will see, each of the planets was heated at the time of its birth, and this primordial heat initially powered extensive volcanic activity, even on our Moon. But, small objects such as the Moon soon cooled off. The larger the planet or moon, the longer it retains its internal heat, and therefore the more we expect to see surface evidence of continuing geological activity. The effect is similar to our own experience with a hot baked potato: the larger the potato, the more slowly it cools. If we want a potato to cool quickly, we cut it into small pieces.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64693">For the most part, the history of volcanic activity on the terrestrial planets conforms to the predictions of this simple theory. The Moon, the smallest of these objects, is a geologically dead world. Although we know less about Mercury, it seems likely that this planet, too, ceased most volcanic activity about the same time the Moon did. Mars represents an intermediate case. It has been much more active than the Moon, but less so than Earth. Earth and Venus, the largest terrestrial planets, still have molten interiors even today, some 4.5 billion years after their birth.</p>

<h2>Rings</h2>

<p class="lt-phys-64146">One characteristic we will find with the Gas Giant planets is rings. A <strong>ring </strong>is a disk of dust or small object orbiting a planet or other body. It is hypothesized that rings can form in one of three ways.</p>

<ol>
    <li class="lt-phys-64146">Material from the solar system's original protoplanetary disk that was within the planet's Roche limit, thus not forming a moon.
    <ul>
        <li class="lt-phys-64146">The <strong>Roche limit </strong>is the minimum distance to which a satellite can approach its primary body without being torn apart by tidal forces due to gravity.</li>
    </ul>
    </li>
    <li class="lt-phys-64146">A moon that broke apart due to Roche limit tidal stresses.</li>
    <li class="lt-phys-64146">Debris from the moon that broke up due to a large impact.</li>
</ol>

<p class="lt-phys-64146">Different forces can influence rings and ring structures. These include other rings, satellites orbiting the planet, and the planet itself.</p>

<p class="lt-phys-64146">There are some objects that have rings but are not Gas Giants. For example, one asteroid has been discovered with rings; this is thought to be due to a collision or collisions. The Martian moon Deimos will eventually break up due to the gravitational tidal forces of Mars and Deimos' orbit getting too close to Mars; one hypothesis is that a ring will be formed from Deimos around Mars at that time.</p>
